## 0. Project shape

Three parts, built in this order:

| Order | Part | Dataset | Status of spec |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | **Part 3** — A/B test analysis | Cookie Cats, flat CSV, ~90k players | **Fully specified below** |
| 2nd | Part 1 — retention & install cohorts | GA4 public sample (Flood-It), BigQuery | Deferred — pending dataset recon |
| 3rd | Part 2 — funnel & monetization | Same GA4 table | Deferred — pending dataset recon |

Build order is deliberately non-numeric. Part 3 is the highest-signal deliverable:
it is the one part where the analysis can support a causal claim, and it is the part
where the reviewer will look for competence. Parts 1 and 2 are presentation-numbered
so the finished repository reads in a sensible order for a reader who arrives cold.

**Dataset for Part 3.** Kaggle `mursideyarkin/mobile-games-ab-testing-cookie-cats`.
One flat CSV. Columns: `userid`, `version` (`gate_30` | `gate_40`),
`sum_gamerounds` (rounds played in the first 14 days), `retention_1` (bool),
`retention_7` (bool).

**The business question behind Part 3.** Cookie Cats places a progression gate that
stops the player and asks them to wait, ask friends, or pay. The gate was at level
30; the experiment moves it to level 40. The question is not "is there a
statistically detectable difference" — it is "should the gate move, and if we move
it, what do we expect to gain".

**One thing about this dataset that shapes every decision below.** There is no
exposure field. Nothing records whether a player ever reached level 30 or level 40.
Every player assigned to `gate_40` who quit at level 6 experienced the same game as
their `gate_30` counterpart, and both are in the denominator. This is an
intention-to-treat design with an unknown and probably large never-exposed
fraction. That fact drives the choice of primary metric (§1.1), the direction of the
interpretation, and the negative-results section (§6). It is stated here once so
that it is not discovered halfway through and written up as a caveat.

## 1. Pre-registration — Part 3

Cookie Cats has hundreds of public notebooks and they disagree about the 1-day
result. The mechanism of that disagreement is not statistical difficulty: it is that
the analyst sees a p-value near 0.05 on two metrics and then chooses which metric
mattered and which tail to test. Everything in this section exists to make that
choice impossible after the fact.

No public analysis of this dataset was consulted while writing this document, and
none may be consulted before Part 3's numbers are final. Reading someone else's
conclusion first turns this pre-registration into theatre.

### 1.1 Primary metric — exactly one

**7-day retention (`retention_7`).** Proportion of assigned players who returned to
the game 7 days after install, over all assigned players in the arm.

Justification, on product grounds:

1. **The treatment has to be delivered before it can be measured.** A gate at level
   30 or 40 only exists for a player who plays far enough to hit it. Reaching level
   30 in a casual match-3 title takes more than one session for most players. On day
   1, most of the assigned population has not yet been exposed to either gate, so
   D1 retention is dominated by players for whom the two arms are literally the same
   product. D7 gives the population a week to reach the gate, so a larger — still
   unknown, but larger — share of each arm has actually received its treatment. The
   primary metric should be the one where the most treatment has been delivered.
2. **The gate is a pacing mechanism, and pacing acts on habit formation, not on
   first impressions.** A gate's product purpose is to stretch the content curve and
   create a moment where a player either commits or churns. That mechanism plays out
   over days, not hours. D7 is the earliest available metric that sits on the right
   side of that mechanism.
3. **D7 is the retention metric the studio would actually act on.** D1 measures
   onboarding and store-listing quality; teams tune it with tutorials and first-run
   experience. D7 is the standard proxy for "did this player form a habit", and it
   is the one that feeds an LTV model. If the gate move is going to be justified to a
   product manager, it is justified in the currency of D7.

Not chosen as primary: `retention_1` (see 1.2), `sum_gamerounds` (heavily skewed,
not a decision variable, and its 14-day window does not line up with either
retention flag — it is reported descriptively in §5 and never tested for the
headline claim).

### 1.2 Secondary metric, and how it is treated differently

**Secondary metric: 1-day retention (`retention_1`), treated as a guardrail, not as
a second hypothesis.**

Concretely, "guardrail" means all of the following, and the difference from the
primary is not cosmetic:

- It gets a point estimate and a 95% bootstrap confidence interval, reported with
  the same precision as the primary.
- It gets **no allocation of alpha** and **no confirmatory significance claim**. The
  write-up may not say "the effect on D1 retention was significant". It may say "the
  95% interval on D1 retention excludes zero", which is a description of an
  interval, not a test decision.
- **It cannot produce a ship recommendation.** A favourable D1 result with a null
  primary does not move the recommendation one millimetre (§1.6, rule R7).
- **It can produce a hold.** If D1 moves adversely and its interval excludes zero,
  it downgrades an otherwise-positive recommendation to "investigate first"
  (§1.6, rule R8). This asymmetry is intentional: a guardrail exists to catch harm,
  not to supply good news.
- A D1 finding that is interesting but not decision-eligible becomes a named
  hypothesis for a future test in §6, not a headline.

### 1.3 Alpha, and sidedness

**Alpha = 0.05, two-sided, allocated entirely to the primary metric.**

Two-sided, because the sign of the effect is genuinely unknown before the fact and
the pre-registration must not pretend otherwise. Both directions are product-plausible:
a later gate removes early friction and could lift retention; equally, a gate is a
pacing device that spaces out content, and removing it early could let players burn
through the content faster and churn sooner. A studio would also act on either
result — a significant harm finding is as decision-relevant as a significant
benefit, because it settles the question and closes the backlog item. A one-sided
test is only honest when one direction is genuinely un-actionable, and that is not
the case here.

**0.05 rather than something stricter,** because this is a single confirmatory test
on a reversible configuration change, not a family of exploratory comparisons and
not a medical decision. Tightening to 0.01 would buy specificity that the decision
does not need while costing sensitivity on the only test that matters.

**Switching to one-sided after seeing the direction of the effect is forbidden**
(§1.7). This is written explicitly because it is the single most common way this
dataset's public analyses manufacture significance.

### 1.4 Minimum detectable effect / action threshold

**MDE = 1.00 percentage point, absolute, on 7-day retention.**

This number does two jobs: it is the effect size the power calculation is evaluated
at (§3), and it is the action threshold in the decision rule (§1.6). Business
reasoning:

- **Moving a gate is cheap to implement and not cheap to own.** It is a config
  change — no art, no new engineering. But it re-tunes the whole progression and
  monetization curve behind it: level difficulty was balanced around a stop at 30,
  the early-game currency sink shifts, and any live-ops calendar built on the old
  pacing has to be re-checked. That is real analyst, QA, and design time, plus the
  cost of having to defend the change if a later metric wobbles. A change with that
  much downstream surface should not be triggered by a hairline effect.
- **Below roughly 1 pp, the effect is smaller than the noise the team lives in.**
  Week-to-week D7 retention on a live casual title moves by more than a point from
  acquisition-mix and seasonality alone. An effect smaller than that cannot be
  verified after launch, cannot be managed, and cannot be defended when someone
  asks six weeks later whether the change worked. An improvement you cannot see in
  production is not an improvement you can act on.
- **1 pp is materially large in relative terms.** Against a D7 base rate in the
  region that casual mobile titles typically see, a full percentage point is a
  mid-single-digit relative change in the retained cohort — and the D7-retained
  cohort is the population that generates essentially all revenue. That is a real
  LTV movement, which is the language the decision is made in.
- **It is plausibly achievable.** A pacing change is expected to move retention by
  fractions of a point to a few points, not by tens. A threshold of 1 pp sits inside
  the range a gate move could actually produce, so the bar is demanding without
  being unmeetable.

The observed control-arm D7 rate is **not** assumed here. It is computed from the
control arm and used as the baseline input to the power calculation (§3). The MDE is
fixed in absolute percentage points precisely so that it does not shift when the
baseline turns out to be different from expectation.

### 1.5 Multiple comparisons

**No formal multiplicity correction is applied. The primary/secondary split carries
the entire burden, and it does so by construction.**

The argument: a family-wise error rate is a property of a family of *decisions*.
Here there is exactly one decision-eligible test — the two-sided z-test on
`retention_7` at alpha = 0.05. `retention_1` has no alpha, cannot trigger a ship,
and is reported as an interval rather than a test (§1.2). A family of one needs no
correction; the FWER of the confirmatory decision is 0.05 by design, not by
adjustment.

**The alternative rejected: Bonferroni at 0.025 per metric.** It loses because it
buys inferential rights to a metric we have already declared cannot drive the
decision, and pays for them by halving the sensitivity of the one test we care
about. Worse, applying a correction across the two metrics *implies both are
decision-eligible* — it quietly restores exactly the two-hypotheses-one-headline
structure this document exists to prevent. A correction here would be a statistical
gesture that made the design less honest.

**The residual risk, stated rather than hidden.** The guardrail interval on D1 is an
uncorrected 95% interval, so across the two metrics there is more than a 5% chance
of seeing at least one interval exclude zero under a true null. That is accepted,
for two reasons: the guardrail is only ever acted on in the adverse direction, and
its consequence is "investigate", not "ship" or "conclude". A false alarm costs a
paragraph of investigation. A false ship costs the studio a re-tuned progression
curve.

**A hierarchical/gated procedure was also considered and rejected:** testing D1 only
if D7 is significant. It is defensible, but it would make the guardrail unavailable
in exactly the case where a guardrail matters most — a null primary with a hidden
D1 regression. The guardrail is therefore always reported, and always
non-confirmatory.

### 1.6 Decision rule — written before the result is known

Notation. Control arm = `gate_30` (the incumbent placement). Variant = `gate_40`
(the proposed move). Primary effect **Δ₇ = D7(gate_40) − D7(gate_30)**, in
percentage points. Guardrail effect **Δ₁ = D1(gate_40) − D1(gate_30)**. `p₇` is the
two-sided pooled-variance z-test p-value on the primary. `CI₇` is the 95% percentile
bootstrap interval on Δ₇ (§4.2). The action threshold is 1.00 pp (§1.4).

The rules are of **three different kinds**, and they are evaluated in **three
separate stages** — not in one flat first-match sweep:

- **Preconditions** (R0, R6) are tested before any outcome branch and can set the
  recommendation on their own.
- **Outcome branches** (R1–R5) are mutually exclusive and exhaustive over the
  remaining space, so **exactly one** fires.
- **Modifiers** (R7–R10) are applied to whichever branch fired. They can qualify a
  recommendation; they can never replace it or create one.

A single first-match ordering over all three kinds is what made the modifiers
unreachable in v1.0: because R1–R5 already partition the outcome space, control
never arrived at R6, R7 or R8, which silently voided the guardrail behaviour
specified in §1.2. Staged evaluation is the fix.

Rule labels are **unchanged from v1.0 deliberately.** `assumptions.md` is
append-only and several of its entries cite these labels; renaming them would
strand those references in a file that cannot be edited to follow.

**Stage 1 — Preconditions.** Evaluated first, in the order listed. If one fires it
sets the recommendation, Stage 3 is not applied, and the Stage 2 branch is still
computed and reported for transparency but does not drive the recommendation. If
neither fires, proceed to Stage 2.

| Rule | Condition | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **R0** | Integrity downgrade triggered (§2.4, §5.2, §5.3) | **No recommendation issued.** Report the analysis in full, clearly labelled as not integrity-established. Stops the recommendation pipeline. |
| **R6** | z-test and bootstrap conflict (§4.3) — `p₇ < 0.05` with `CI₇` containing zero, or `p₇ ≥ 0.05` with `CI₇` excluding zero | **Keep the gate at level 30**, per the conservative branch in §4.3, and report the conflict itself as a finding with its Monte Carlo error. Stops the recommendation pipeline. |

**Stage 2 — Outcome branch.** Reached when Stage 1 is clean. Exactly one of R1–R5
fires; the self-verification step (§7.7) asserts that. Because R6 has already
removed every case where the test and the interval disagree, significance and the
interval agree throughout this stage. Boundary conventions: a `CI₇` lower bound of
exactly +1.00 pp counts as **not** clearing the threshold (R2), and an interval
endpoint of exactly ±1.00 pp counts as **within** the threshold (R4).

| Rule | Condition | Recommendation, fixed in advance |
|---|---|---|
| **R1** | `p₇ < 0.05` and lower bound of `CI₇` > +1.00 pp | **Move the gate to level 40.** An actionable benefit is both detected and bounded away from the threshold. |
| **R2** | `p₇ < 0.05`, Δ₇ > 0, lower bound of `CI₇` ≤ +1.00 pp | **Keep the gate at level 30.** A real effect was detected but the interval does not establish it clears the action bar. The follow-up is a revenue-powered test, not a re-run of this one. |
| **R3** | `p₇ < 0.05` and Δ₇ < 0 | **Keep the gate at level 30.** Report the estimated retention cost of the move with its interval; the backlog item is closed on retention grounds. |
| **R4** | `p₇ ≥ 0.05` and all of `CI₇` lies within ±1.00 pp | **Keep the gate at level 30 — and report this as a positive finding.** The experiment rules out any D7 effect large enough to act on. This is practical equivalence at the action threshold, not a failed test. |
| **R5** | `p₇ ≥ 0.05` and `CI₇` extends beyond ±1.00 pp on either side | **Keep the gate at level 30, inconclusive at the action threshold.** The experiment neither detected nor ruled out an actionable effect. This must **not** be written as "there is no difference between the arms". |

**Stage 3 — Modifiers.** Applied to the branch that fired in Stage 2, and only when
Stage 1 was clean. Exactly one of R7–R10 applies, and the write-up states which —
including when it is a no-op, so a reader can see the guardrail was checked.

| Rule | Condition | Effect on the Stage 2 recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **R7** | Δ₁ > 0 with a 95% interval excluding zero (favourable guardrail) | **Unchanged.** Explicit no-op: a positive D1 result never upgrades R2–R5 into a move. Report it, and carry it into §6.4 as a follow-up hypothesis if it is interesting. |
| **R8** | Δ₁ < 0 with a 95% interval excluding zero (adverse guardrail) **and** the Stage 2 branch is R1 | **Downgraded to conditional: move the gate only after the D1 regression is explained.** A D7 gain bought with a D1 loss may be a composition effect rather than a genuine improvement. |
| **R9** | Δ₁ < 0 with a 95% interval excluding zero **and** the Stage 2 branch is R2, R3, R4 or R5 | **Unchanged** — the recommendation is already the conservative one. Report the regression and name it in §6.4 as a follow-up hypothesis. |
| **R10** | `CI₁` contains zero (quiet guardrail) | **Unchanged.** State that the guardrail was checked and was quiet. |

No modifier can produce a move recommendation. Only R1 can, and only R8 can qualify
it.

**Reachability trace.** Every stage and every modifier is reachable; here are six
cases traced through the structure, and this table is part of the deliverable so a
reviewer can check the claim rather than take it.

| Case | Stage 1 | Stage 2 | Stage 3 | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) `p₇ < 0.05`, `CI₇` lower bound +1.6 pp, `CI₁` contains zero | clean | **R1** | **R10** | Move the gate to level 40 |
| (b) same as (a), but Δ₁ < 0 with `CI₁` excluding zero | clean | **R1** | **R8** | Move only after the D1 regression is explained |
| (c) `p₇ ≥ 0.05`, `CI₇` entirely inside ±1.00 pp | clean | **R4** | R7, R9 or R10 as observed | Keep at level 30 — reported as practical equivalence |
| (d) `p₇ < 0.05` but `CI₇` contains zero | **R6** fires | computed and reported (would be R2), does not drive | not applied | Keep at level 30, conflict reported as a finding |
| (e) SRM p-value < 0.001 | **R0** fires | computed and reported, does not drive | not applied | No recommendation issued |
| (f) `p₇ ≥ 0.05`, `CI₇` wider than ±1.00 pp, Δ₁ < 0 with `CI₁` excluding zero | clean | **R5** | **R9** | Keep at level 30, inconclusive; D1 regression named in §6.4 |

Case (b) is the one that could not fire at all under v1.0. Cases (d) and (e) show
why the preconditions must sit ahead of the branches rather than after them.

The recommendation is derived mechanically from these three stages. The write-up
quotes the full path — precondition state, branch, modifier — by rule name. No other
consideration, whether the effect on `sum_gamerounds`, the appeal of a positive
story, or what other analyses of this dataset concluded, may alter it.

### 1.7 Pre-registration integrity — what would invalidate this document

Part 3 is invalid, and must be labelled as such, if any of these occurs:

- The primary metric is changed after any retention statistic has been computed.
- The test is switched from two-sided to one-sided at any point.
- A multiplicity correction is added or removed after seeing a p-value.
- The MDE or alpha is moved after seeing an effect size.
- The `sum_gamerounds` outlier is dropped from the primary analysis (§5.1).
- Power is computed at the observed effect size and presented as the study's power
  (§3).
- A public Cookie Cats analysis is consulted before the numbers are final.

**Verifiability requirement.** `ARCHITECTURE.md` and `assumptions.md` must be
committed to git **before the first commit that touches `src/`**, and the Part 3
report must cite the commit SHA of this file as it stood at analysis time. A
pre-registration that cannot be shown to predate the analysis is not a
pre-registration.

## 2. Sample ratio mismatch protocol — decided in advance

The group sizes in this dataset are not exactly equal. The protocol below is written
so that whatever the SRM test returns, the response is already determined. The
actual counts have not been looked at.

### 2.1 Expected allocation

**1:1 (0.5 / 0.5) between `gate_30` and `gate_40`.** The dataset is documented as a
two-arm split with no stated weighting, and 1:1 is the default for a two-arm
retention test because it maximises power for a fixed total sample. This is an
assumption, recorded as `A-008`, and it is the null the SRM test is run against.

**This is a permanent unknown** (§9, question 2): no public documentation states the
design ratio, so the assumption can never be confirmed from this dataset. The
1:1 default is therefore kept, and the report carries a **required conditional
statement**, in the SRM subsection and not in a footnote: *if the original design
was weighted rather than 1:1, the SRM p-value as computed here is uninterpretable,
and with it the integrity claim that rests on it.* That sentence appears whether or
not the test trips, because its truth does not depend on the outcome.

### 2.2 The test

**Primary SRM test: two-sided exact binomial test** of the observed `gate_30` count
against a hypothesised proportion of 0.5, with n as defined immediately below.

**Denominator — the literal rule.** SRM n is the number of rows in the **raw input**
whose `version` value is exactly `gate_30` or `gate_40` after coercion against the
token sets in §5.4. This is the **assignment population**, and it is fixed before any exclusion
is applied. No other filter runs first: duplicate `userid`s, nulls in either
retention flag, and the extreme `sum_gamerounds` row are **all counted in the SRM
denominator**, including rows that are excluded from the metric analysis moments
later.

The reason is the principle in §5.1: a cleaning judgement must never sit upstream of
an integrity test. A row is counted here because it was *assigned* — that is the
only property SRM is about — and what happens to it afterwards, for reasons that
have nothing to do with assignment, cannot be allowed to move the count. Execution
order enforces this: the SRM test runs at step 8 of §7.7, before duplicate and
missing-value handling at steps 10 and 11.

Rows whose `version` is **missing** — an empty field or a recognised missing token
(§5.4) — are **unassignable** and enter neither arm: they were never assigned, so
there is nothing for SRM to test about them. They are reported separately with their
count and share, and if they exceed **0.1%** of raw rows the §2.4 integrity
downgrade applies (R0), because a missing assignment is a failure of the assignment
or logging mechanism itself, the same class of fault as a cross-arm duplicate and
given the same tight threshold.

A `version` value that is **present but is neither `gate_30` nor `gate_40`** is a
different case and is **not** unassignable: it is a structural fault (preamble to
§5) and stops the run at §7.7 step 5. It never reaches the 0.1% threshold, and that
is deliberate — a third arm label means this is not the two-arm experiment §1
pre-registers, so no version of this analysis applies to the file at all, and
counting such rows as a small percentage of an otherwise valid experiment would be
the wrong response to the wrong file. v1.1 conflated the two cases by defining
unassignable as "null or outside the whitelist" while §7.7 hard-stopped on the
second half of that set; this is the correction.

**Differential exclusion is reported, not tested.** After the metric populations are
built, the post-exclusion arm counts are reported next to the SRM result as a
**descriptive** check on whether exclusions fell unevenly between arms. No test and
no threshold is attached to it, because a second SRM-style test on a cleaned
population would reintroduce exactly the cleaning-upstream-of-integrity problem this
rule exists to prevent. Uneven exclusion is instead handled as a reported finding
under §5.3's differential-missingness requirement.

Exact binomial rather than chi-square goodness-of-fit as the primary, for one
reason: it removes the continuity-correction question entirely. At n ≈ 90,000 the
two agree to more decimal places than anyone will read, so the choice costs nothing
and eliminates a decision that would otherwise have to be defended.

The **chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic (1 df) is reported alongside** it, because
it is the form a reviewer with an experimentation background expects to see, and
agreement between the two is itself a small reassurance.

The SRM result is reported **unconditionally**, with its exact p-value, whether or
not it trips. An SRM check that is only mentioned when it fails is not a check.

### 2.3 Failure threshold

**SRM failure is declared at p < 0.001.**

Not alpha (0.05), and the reason is that the SRM test is not a hypothesis of
interest — it is a data-quality alarm on a nuisance parameter, and alarms are tuned
for specificity, not for the conventional 5%:

- At 0.05, about one in twenty perfectly clean experiments would trip an alarm whose
  consequence is that the headline result gets withheld. That false-alarm rate is
  unacceptable for a gate on the deliverable.
- The failure mode SRM is designed to catch — a broken assignment service, a logging
  filter applied to one arm, a bot-traffic asymmetry — produces gross imbalance, not
  marginal imbalance. At n ≈ 90,000 the test has overwhelming power against those,
  so tightening to 0.001 sacrifices almost nothing in sensitivity to real bugs.
- The costs are asymmetric. A false alarm discards a valid experiment. A missed
  *small* imbalance is something an intention-to-treat proportion comparison
  tolerates well, because it changes the arm weights, not the within-arm rates.

0.001 is therefore the operating point where the alarm still catches everything it
was built for while almost never firing on a clean split.

### 2.4 What happens if SRM trips

Decided now: **the analysis neither stops nor continues unchanged. It continues with
the recommendation withheld.** Specifically:

1. The full primary and guardrail analysis is computed and reported as specified.
   Nothing is suppressed.
2. **Decision rule R0 fires: no recommendation is issued.** The report states that
   the experiment's assignment integrity is not established and that no gate
   decision can rest on it.
3. A diagnostic subsection is added, covering: the observed counts and the implied
   allocation, the magnitude and direction of the imbalance, and the direction the
   imbalance would bias the estimate if the excess rows differ systematically from
   the rest.
4. That subsection must also state the hard limit: **this dataset contains no
   pre-treatment covariates.** `sum_gamerounds` and both retention flags are all
   post-assignment outcomes. There is therefore no randomisation check available
   beyond the arm counts themselves, and no covariate-balance table can be built.
   A tripped SRM cannot be diagnosed further from this data.
5. The downgrade is **permanent for this deliverable**, because the input is a static
   public CSV: there is no assignment log, no pipeline, and no upstream owner to
   query. There is no path from "SRM tripped" back to "resolved".

**Explicitly forbidden if SRM trips:** re-randomisation, post-stratification or
inverse-propensity reweighting to restore balance, trimming rows from the larger arm,
and re-running the SRM test on any subset. Each of these repairs the symptom by
introducing a selection process after assignment, which is a larger problem than the
one being fixed.

**If p ≥ 0.001:** proceed unchanged, and report the p-value anyway.

## 3. Power analysis specification

**What is computed:** power for a two-sided two-proportion z-test at alpha = 0.05,
using the normal approximation with pooled variance under the null, on the
**primary metric only** (`retention_7`).

**Inputs, all stated in the report next to the result:**

- Baseline proportion: the **observed control-arm (`gate_30`) D7 retention rate**.
  Not an assumed figure.
- Arm sizes: the **observed** n in each arm, not the nominal 1:1 split.
- Alpha: 0.05, two-sided.
- Effect size for the power figure: **1.00 pp absolute** (the pre-registered MDE,
  §1.4).
- Power targets for the inverse calculation: **0.80** and **0.95**.

**Reported as** a three-row table:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Power to detect a 1.00 pp absolute difference, at observed n and observed baseline | (computed) |
| Smallest absolute difference detectable at 80% power | (computed, pp) |
| Smallest absolute difference detectable at 95% power | (computed, pp) |

plus **one required sentence** comparing the 80%-power detectable effect against the
1.00 pp action threshold, and stating which of the two is larger. That comparison is
the whole point of the exercise: if the sample can detect effects smaller than the
threshold we would act on, then a null result is genuinely informative and rule R4
becomes available. If it cannot, then a null result is uninformative and rule R5
applies. Which of those two worlds we are in must be established from the sample
size, not from the outcome.

A power figure for the guardrail metric may be reported, labelled descriptive.

**This is a fixed-n dataset.** The CSV has the rows it has; no more can be collected
and the experiment cannot be extended. The power calculation is therefore
**descriptive of the sensitivity this sample happens to have** — it answers "what
could this experiment have seen", and it is presented before the results so that it
reads as a property of the design rather than an excuse for the outcome. It is
**not** a sample-size decision, and the report may not phrase it as one.

**Banned:** computing power at the *observed* effect size and presenting it as the
study's power. Observed-effect post-hoc power is a monotone re-expression of the
p-value; it carries no information the p-value does not, and it is routinely used to
dress a null result up as a near-miss. The only effect sizes the power calculation
is evaluated at are 1.00 pp and the two inverse-solved values above.

## 4. Estimation and inference plan

### 4.1 Two-proportion z-test

**Variant: pooled-proportion (common-p) variance estimator, two-sided, no continuity
correction.**

- **Pooled variance for the test.** Under H₀ the two arms share a single proportion,
  so the standard error is estimated from the combined success rate. This is the
  correct null variance and it is the standard form of the two-proportion z-test.
- **Unpooled (Wald) variance for the confidence interval.** Estimating the
  *difference* is a different problem from testing whether it is zero; under the
  alternative the two arms do not share a proportion, so each arm's variance is
  estimated from its own data. This asymmetry — pooled SE for the p-value, unpooled
  SE for the interval — is deliberate and must be stated in the report, because a
  reader who notices two different standard errors and no explanation will assume
  it is a bug.
- **No continuity correction.** Yates' correction is a conservative adjustment for
  small expected cell counts. With tens of thousands of observations per cell the
  normal approximation is accurate far beyond the precision of the decision, the
  correction would shift the p-value by an amount invisible at the reported
  precision, and applying it would make the test inconsistent with the uncorrected
  interval reported next to it. A single-source-of-truth argument, not a taste one.

**Estimand.** The intention-to-treat effect on assigned players: the difference in
the proportion retained at day 7 among **all** players assigned to each arm,
regardless of whether they ever reached a gate. This is what the data identifies.
It is explicitly **not** the effect on players who actually encountered the gate
(the complier-average or treated-population effect), which is not identifiable here
because no exposure field exists. The report names the estimand in these terms.

**Reporting precision.** Retention rates and effects to two decimal places in
percentage points. Exact p-values, to three significant figures — never "p < 0.05",
never rounded to 0, and no asterisk/star significance notation anywhere in the
repository. Every reported figure comes from a generated output file (§7.5).

### 4.2 Bootstrap

- **Quantity bootstrapped:** Δ₇ = mean(`retention_7` | `gate_40`) −
  mean(`retention_7` | `gate_30`), expressed in percentage points. The identical
  procedure produces the guardrail interval on Δ₁ using `retention_1`. Nothing else
  is bootstrapped unless a new `assumptions.md` entry says so.
- **Resamples: 10,000.** Enough that the Monte Carlo error on the 2.5% and 97.5%
  percentiles is small relative to the two-decimal reporting precision, and cheap
  enough on a 90k-row frame to re-run freely.
- **Resampling scheme: stratified by arm.** Each arm is resampled with replacement
  to its own observed size, so every replicate preserves the observed allocation.
  Pooling the two arms and resampling the combination would destroy the arm
  structure and estimate the wrong sampling distribution.
- **RNG seed: 20260910.** A literal, defined once (§7.5), imported everywhere.
- **CI method: percentile.** The statistic is a difference of two proportions —
  smooth, essentially unbiased, and with a sampling distribution that is very close
  to symmetric at these sample sizes. BCa's bias-correction and acceleration terms
  are designed for biased or skewed statistics (a ratio, or a mean of a heavy-tailed
  variable); here they would adjust for skew that is not present, at the cost of an
  extra jackknife pass and a more complex method to defend. The **basic
  (reverse-percentile)** interval is rejected for the same reason — it applies a
  bias correction we have no reason to believe is needed, and it can place an
  endpoint outside the feasible range for a proportion difference.
- **Consistency rule for later work:** if any interval is ever reported on a
  `sum_gamerounds` statistic, **BCa is required there**, because that variable is
  heavily right-skewed and percentile intervals on its mean would be
  miscalibrated. The method follows the shape of the statistic, which is why
  percentile is right here and would be wrong there.
- **Monte Carlo error must be reported** alongside the interval (the bootstrap
  standard error of the estimate), so a reader can tell resampling noise from
  substance. This is also what §4.3 needs.

### 4.3 Reconciling the z-test and the bootstrap

They share a point estimate, so they cannot disagree about the sign of the effect.
"Disagreement" means exactly one of two things: `p₇ < 0.05` while `CI₇` contains
zero, or `p₇ ≥ 0.05` while `CI₇` excludes zero. Decided in advance:

1. **Both are reported, prominently, side by side.** Picking whichever is friendlier
   is the failure this document exists to prevent.
2. **Roles are fixed:** the z-test is the *confirmatory instrument* — it is what
   alpha was pre-registered against, so it and it alone decides the significance
   condition in §1.6. The bootstrap is the *estimation instrument* — it decides the
   action-threshold comparison. The decision rule already requires both a
   significant test **and** an interval clearing the threshold, so a conflicted result
   cannot reach R1 at all: it is caught by **precondition R6 in Stage 1 of §1.6**,
   ahead of every outcome branch, and resolves to the conservative branch — keep the
   gate at level 30.
3. **The conflict is itself a reported finding**, not a footnote. The report must
   state which of the two conditions occurred and name the likely cause: a p-value
   sitting near 0.05, where the discrepancy between an asymptotic pooled-variance
   test and a percentile interval is on the order of the bootstrap's own Monte Carlo
   error. The reported Monte Carlo error (§4.2) is what lets the reader judge that.
4. **Re-running the bootstrap with a different seed or a higher resample count to
   resolve a conflict is forbidden.** That is p-hacking with extra steps. If 10,000
   resamples prove genuinely insufficient, the fix is an `assumptions.md` entry
   changing the resample count for *all* estimates, applied before results are read.

## 5. Data handling policy

**Two classes of fault.** Every fault this section deals with falls into one of two
classes, and the class alone determines the response. The line is drawn once, here,
because §5.3, §5.4, §2.2 and §7.7 all rest on it — and in v1.0 and v1.1 they rested
on it inconsistently, which is what made §5.3's missing-value policy unreachable.

- **Structural faults — the run stops.** The file is not the file this document
  describes. Detected at §7.7 step 5, before any statistic is computed: hard stop,
  nothing reported, no outputs written.
- **Data-quality faults — counted, reported, thresholded.** The file is the right
  file and some values are absent or duplicated. Never fatal: each is counted,
  disclosed next to every statistic it touches, and triggers the §2.4 integrity
  downgrade only past a stated threshold.

**The principle: an absent value is a data-quality fault; an unrecognised present
value is a structural fault.** A missing value is self-describing — we know exactly
what we do not know, and §5.3 has a defined, disclosed response to it. An
unrecognised non-empty value is not self-describing: acting on it means guessing
what the producer meant, and a guess about the meaning of an outcome or an arm label
is the imputation §5.3 forbids wearing a different hat. And in a fixed public CSV an
unrecognised token almost always means the wrong file, a mangled export, a wrong
delimiter, or a different revision of the dataset — so the remedy is to fetch the
right file, never to clean this one.

| Case | Class | Response |
|---|---|---|
| `version` missing (empty field, or a recognised missing token) | Data quality | Row is unassignable; counted and reported; 0.1% downgrade threshold (§2.2) |
| `version` present but neither `gate_30` nor `gate_40` | **Structural** | Hard stop. A third arm label means this is not the two-arm experiment §1 pre-registers |
| `retention_1` / `retention_7` missing | Data quality | Excluded from that metric only; counted; 0.5% downgrade threshold (§5.3) |
| `retention_1` / `retention_7` present but in neither the true nor the false token set | **Structural** | Hard stop. There is no defensible mapping, and inventing one fabricates an outcome |
| `sum_gamerounds` missing | Data quality | Excluded from engagement descriptives only; counted (§5.3) |
| `sum_gamerounds` present but not a non-negative integer | **Structural** | Hard stop |
| Duplicate `userid` | Data quality | Handled by case; counted; 0.1% cross-arm downgrade threshold (§5.2) |
| The extreme `sum_gamerounds` value | Neither — a valid observation | Kept and disclosed (§5.1) |

For this dataset none of the structural cases is expected: the CSV is a well-known
complete export with five columns and two arms. The checks exist because that is a
claim to be **established rather than assumed**, and because a check that only runs
when it fails is not a check (§2.2).

One consequence, stated so it is not a surprise: the structural gate is a **single
step ahead of everything else** rather than one check per column at the point of
use. If a retention column's tokens were validated after the SRM test, a structural
stop would discard an SRM result that had already been computed and reported — a
number published from a file that turned out not to be the right file. Validating
all four columns before any statistic exists removes that possibility.

### 5.1 The extreme `sum_gamerounds` outlier

The dataset contains one player with an extreme `sum_gamerounds` value. Most public
notebooks drop it as a first step.

**Decision: keep it in the primary analysis. Report a both-ways sensitivity check on
`sum_gamerounds` statistics only. The retained version is primary in all cases.**

Reasoning:

- **Intention-to-treat.** The row is a real randomised assignment. Removing rows
  from a randomised experiment *after* assignment, on the basis of an outcome
  variable, is a selection process applied post-randomisation — the exact thing
  randomisation exists to prevent. It does not matter that the selection looks
  innocuous; the rule "drop rows whose outcomes look strange" is not defensible in
  general, and this dataset gives no reason to make an exception.
- **It perturbs the SRM count.** Dropping the row changes an arm's assigned count by
  one, which means a data-cleaning judgement call now feeds into the
  randomisation-integrity check in §2. A cleaning step should never be upstream of
  an integrity test. §2.2 makes that principle operational rather than rhetorical:
  the SRM denominator is the assignment population, fixed before any exclusion, so
  this row is counted there regardless of what happens to it downstream — and the
  same protection applies to every other row §5.2 and §5.3 remove.
- **The primary metric is binary and the outlier is not in it.** The outlier is
  extreme in `sum_gamerounds`. `retention_7` is a boolean; one player contributes
  exactly one success or failure regardless of how many rounds they played. A single
  row can move an arm's proportion by at most 1/n — on the order of 0.002 pp, three
  orders of magnitude below the 1.00 pp action threshold.

**What keeping it can affect:** the mean of `sum_gamerounds`, any interval on that
mean, the shape and axis scaling of engagement distribution plots, and any
correlation-style descriptive statistic involving rounds played.

**What it cannot affect:** the primary and guardrail proportions beyond ~1/n, the
z-test conclusion, the bootstrap interval at reported precision, the SRM p-value in
any material way (one row in ~90,000), and therefore the recommendation.

**Required handling in the report:** the value is disclosed explicitly with its arm,
and described as implausible-but-unverifiable — a value of that size is more likely
a logging artefact or an automated client than a human, but this dataset contains
nothing that can distinguish those, and the honest statement is that it cannot be
adjudicated. Wherever a `sum_gamerounds` mean or interval is reported, the
with-and-without figures appear together, alongside the median and a stated
winsorised or trimmed summary, so no engagement claim rests on a single row.
Silent deletion is a defect.

### 5.2 Duplicate `userid`s

Expected: none. Uniqueness is **asserted**, not assumed, and the assertion result is
reported.

If duplicates exist, they are not silently de-duplicated — a duplicate `userid` in
an A/B export means either double-logging or a user who appears in both arms, and
those have different consequences:

- **Same arm, identical metric values:** treated as an export artefact. Keep one row,
  report the count, append an `assumptions.md` entry with the count.
- **Same arm, conflicting metric values:** the row cannot be resolved from this data.
  Exclude the `userid` entirely from the metric analysis, report the count.
- **Across arms:** these users are cross-contaminated — they cannot be attributed to
  a treatment. Exclude them from **both** arms, report the count and the arms
  involved, and state that ITT is compromised for that subset.
- **Threshold:** if cross-arm duplicates exceed **0.1%** of total rows, the
  integrity downgrade of §2.4 applies (rule R0, no recommendation). Below that,
  the analysis proceeds with the exclusions disclosed next to every affected
  statistic.
- **These exclusions do not alter the SRM denominator** (§2.2). Duplicates can
  trigger the integrity downgrade through their own threshold above, which is a
  different mechanism from changing the SRM count — the count they would have
  changed is already fixed by the time this check runs.

### 5.3 Missing values (nulls)

Expected: none. **No imputation, under any circumstances** — imputing an outcome in
a randomised experiment fabricates the thing being measured.

**Everything in this subsection is a data-quality fault** (see the preamble to §5):
counted, reported, thresholded, never fatal. That is only possible because coercion
admits an explicit *missing* state instead of raising on it — see §5.4, which is the
change that makes this policy reachable at all. Anything that is neither a
recognised value nor a recognised missing marker was already stopped at §7.7 step 5
and never arrives here.

- A **missing** `version` — an empty field or a recognised missing token (§5.4) —
  makes the row unassignable: exclude it from everything and report the count and
  share. Unassignable rows enter neither arm of the SRM denominator, and the
  **0.1%** threshold in §2.2 applies to them. A `version` value that is *present*
  but is not an arm label is not a null and not unassignable: it is structural, it
  stops the run, and it never reaches this threshold.
- Any missing `retention_1` or `retention_7`: exclude the row from **that metric
  only**, not from the whole analysis, and print the exclusion count adjacent to
  every statistic computed from that metric, including the denominators actually
  used. This branch is reachable: step 6 of §7.7 coerces a recognised missing marker
  to the missing state and continues, so the run arrives at step 11 with the missing
  counts intact.
- A null in `sum_gamerounds` has no effect on the retention analysis; it is reported
  and excluded from engagement descriptives only.
- **Threshold:** if nulls in the primary metric exceed **0.5%** of either arm, the
  integrity downgrade of §2.4 applies. Differential missingness between arms is
  itself a finding and must be reported even below the threshold.
- **Null-driven exclusions do not alter the SRM denominator** (§2.2). They change
  the per-metric denominators only, and those are reported separately alongside the
  descriptive differential-exclusion check.

### 5.4 Token sets and type coercion

Every column is read as text (§7.7 step 3) and validated against **enumerated token
sets** before anything is computed. The sets are:

- **`retention_1`, `retention_7`** — a **true set**, a **false set**, and a
  **missing set**. The true and false sets cover the spellings a CSV export of this
  dataset can produce (`True`/`False` and `1`/`0`). The missing set is the empty
  field, a whitespace-only field, and the conventional missing spellings a text
  reader can hand back: `NA`, `N/A`, `NaN`, `nan`, `null`, `NULL`, `None`.
  Comparison is on the trimmed value and is case-sensitive except where a spelling
  is listed in both cases above.
- **`version`** — the arm set {`gate_30`, `gate_40`}, plus the same missing set.
- **`sum_gamerounds`** — a non-negative integer, or a value in the missing set.

Rules:

- **A value outside all of its column's sets is a structural fault and hard-stops
  the run** at §7.7 step 5. It does not silently become `False`, it is not dropped,
  and it is not counted against any threshold. The exception names the column, the
  offending value, and how many rows carry it.
- **A value in the missing set coerces to an explicit missing state and the run
  continues.** This is the correction that makes §5.3 executable: under v1.0 and
  v1.1 coercion raised on anything outside the true and false sets, and a null is
  outside them, so every retention null terminated the run and the whole
  missing-value policy was unreachable.
- **Truthiness coercion is banned.** Casting the string `"False"` to a boolean in
  Python yields `True`, which produces a wrong retention rate that looks perfectly
  plausible and passes every eyeball check. This is the single highest-consequence
  silent bug available in this dataset, and the enumerated sets exist specifically
  to make it impossible.
- **Post-coercion assertions**, whose results are reported:
  - each retention column's **non-missing** values form exactly the set
    {`True`, `False`}. This replaces v1.1's "exactly two distinct values", which is
    false by construction once the column is three-state — the two assertions cannot
    both hold, and the three-state one is the one that matches the policy;
  - for each retention column, `count(True) + count(False) + count(missing)` equals
    the raw row count;
  - the count of each coerced state equals the total count of its source tokens
    before coercion, with the missing state summed across every missing spelling
    that actually occurred;
  - `version` contains exactly {`gate_30`, `gate_40`} among its non-missing values,
    and the unassignable count equals its missing count;
  - `sum_gamerounds` is a non-negative integer type among its non-missing values,
    with no negatives.
- Any assertion failure is a hard stop, not a warning — and it is structural by
  definition, because it means the coercion did not preserve the file it was given.

### 5.5 Input provenance

The raw CSV is **not committed** to the repository (redistribution of a Kaggle
dataset is not ours to grant). Instead, `assumptions.md` records: the dataset slug,
the file name, the row count, and the **SHA-256 of the CSV** as used. `data/raw/` is
git-ignored, and the README documents the fetch step. The clean-checkout rule (§7.5)
verifies the checksum before running anything, so "reproduces exactly" is a claim a
reader can check rather than take on trust.

## 6. Negative results — a required deliverable

Part 3 ends with a top-level section titled **"What this experiment does not
support"**, placed **after the results and before the recommendation**. It is not a
caveats footnote, not a bulleted "limitations" list at the end, and not a line in the
README. Placing it before the recommendation is deliberate: the reader should know
the boundaries of the claim before they read the claim.

It must contain four labelled subsections.

**6.1 Not answerable from this data at all.** At minimum:

- **Revenue and LTV.** There are no monetization fields. A gate is fundamentally a
  monetization instrument, so the experiment cannot speak to the variable the gate
  is actually tuning. A retention-neutral gate move could still be revenue-positive
  or revenue-negative, and nothing here would show it.
- **Anything past day 7.** Retention flags stop at D7. `sum_gamerounds` covers 14
  days, so the two windows do not even align. D14, D30, and long-run churn are out
  of reach, and a pacing change is precisely the kind of intervention whose effect
  could appear or reverse after the first week.
- **The effect on players who actually met the gate.** No exposure field exists.
  This is the central limitation and must be stated as such: the estimate is ITT
  over all assigned players, an unknown and probably large share of whom never
  reached level 30, let alone 40. Those players dilute the true effect on exposed
  players toward zero by an unknown factor. **Therefore a null result here is not
  evidence that gate placement does not matter to players who reach it** — and the
  report must say that sentence, or its equivalent, out loud.
- **Cannibalisation and mechanism.** Whether a retention change comes from reduced
  friction, altered pacing, changed session length, or shifted difficulty is not
  distinguishable with two boolean flags and a round count.

**6.2 Answered only under stated assumptions.** Each item names the assumption and
the `assumptions.md` entry it lives in — at minimum: which arm is the incumbent
(A-001), the intended 1:1 allocation that the SRM test is run against (A-008), and
the treatment of the extreme outlier (A-021).

**6.3 Confounds and unverifiable properties that remain.** At minimum:

- **Unknown randomisation unit** — user, device, or install. If it is device or
  install, a single person can appear in both arms and ITT is contaminated in a way
  the duplicate check cannot see.
- **Unknown assignment timing** relative to install, so it cannot be confirmed that
  assignment preceded any measured behaviour.
- **No pre-treatment covariates**, so no randomisation or balance check is possible
  beyond arm counts (see §2.4). Acquisition-channel mix, geography, device tier, and
  platform could differ between arms and it would be undetectable here.
- **Unknown calendar window.** Day-of-week effects, a live-ops event, a store
  feature, or a marketing burst overlapping one arm's install cohort cannot be
  ruled out.
- **Reinstall and reassignment behaviour** is unspecified, so a churned player who
  returned under the other arm's configuration is invisible.
- **Novelty and learning effects** cannot be separated from steady-state effects in
  a single fixed window.
- **Unknown game version and unknown level-difficulty tuning** at the time of the
  test, which determine how hard level 30 actually was — the treatment is defined by
  a level number, and a level number is not a fixed quantity of difficulty across
  versions.

**6.4 What would be required to answer the business question properly.** Every item
must name a specific measurement, not a general aspiration. "Further research is
needed" is not an acceptable entry. At minimum:

- **Exposure instrumentation:** an event recording whether and when each player
  reached the gate, which converts the ITT estimate into an effect on the exposed
  population and makes the dilution measurable rather than unknown.
- **Revenue per user over a longer horizon**, so the experiment is powered on the
  metric the gate exists to move.
- **Retention at D14 and D30**, to see whether a first-week effect persists or
  reverses.
- **Level-progression telemetry** — the distribution of highest level reached — to
  see where players actually stall, which is what determines whether 30 or 40 is
  even the right pair of candidates.
- **A multi-arm test** with at least one further placement (for example a third arm
  at a level well beyond 40), because a two-arm test cannot detect a non-monotone
  response and "40 beats 30" does not imply "later is better".
- **Named follow-up hypotheses**, including any interesting guardrail movement from
  §1.2, written as testable statements rather than as observations.

**Form requirements.** Every item is written as a claim about what the data can and
cannot do, in plain language a product manager can act on. Any headline claim in the
README must be checkable against this section without contradiction. If a reader
finishes this section believing something the results do not support, the section
has failed.

This differs from `sql/` deliberately, and the reason is written here so it does not
read as sloppiness: **SQL files are executed, Python modules are imported.** For
something executed in sequence, an ordinal prefix is the clearest possible signal of
order. For something imported, a leading digit is an illegal Python identifier, and
faking order with `01_load.py` forces `importlib` gymnastics and makes the code
harder to test. Execution order for Part 3 therefore lives in exactly one place —
the entrypoint — which is a better place for it anyway, because it is a file a
reviewer can read top to bottom.

Hand-editing an output file is a defect.

**Reports.** `reports/partX_<slug>.md`, one file per part.

### 7.3 The SQL / pandas boundary — a decision, not an inconsistency

**Rule for Parts 1 and 2:** every aggregation, join, filter, cohort assignment, and
window function runs in **BigQuery SQL**. Python's role is to execute a query,
receive an already-aggregated result of at most a few thousand rows, and render it.
No `groupby`, no `merge`, no `resample` on GA4 data.

Why:

- The GA4 events table is nested and date-sharded and far larger than local memory.
  Pulling raw events down to aggregate them locally is the wrong shape of work and
  would not survive a scale-up.
- The aggregation logic *is* the analytical claim. A cohort definition or a retention
  window belongs somewhere a reviewer can read it as a single statement, in the
  language the warehouse speaks.
- Cohort definitions must exist in exactly one auditable place. Re-deriving them in
  pandas guarantees they drift.

**Rule for Part 3:** the analysis is **Python**, and this is required by the shape of
the problem rather than tolerated as an exception:

- The input is a single flat CSV of roughly 90,000 rows. It fits in memory with room
  to spare, and there is no warehouse involved at all.
- The deliverable is not an aggregation — it is a statistical procedure: a
  pooled-variance z-test, an exact binomial SRM test, a seeded stratified bootstrap
  with 10,000 replicates, and a power curve. None of these exist in SQL without
  reimplementing distribution functions in SQL, which would be dramatically *less*
  auditable, not more.

**The single underlying principle:** computation happens where the data lives and
where the operation is naturally expressible. Applied to a partitioned
warehouse-scale event table that yields SQL; applied to a small flat file and an
inference procedure it yields Python. The two rules are one rule meeting two
different inputs.

Two corollaries, stated so nobody "fixes" the apparent mismatch:

- It is **not permitted** to load GA4 raw events into pandas for consistency with
  Part 3.
- It is **not permitted** to push the bootstrap or the z-test into SQL for
  consistency with Parts 1 and 2.

Descriptive aggregation *inside* Part 3 — arm counts, retention rates,

### 7.4 Notebooks

No notebook may be the source of any number in the repository. If a `.ipynb` is ever
produced, it is a presentation artefact generated from the scripts, and the scripts
remain the source of truth. Notebooks are excluded from the reproducible path

### 7.5 Reproducibility

- **Python:** minor version **3.12**, pinned in `.python-version`. The exact patch
  version used is recorded in `assumptions.md` at repository initialisation. The
  minor version may not change without a new `assumptions.md` entry.
- **Dependencies:** `requirements.txt` pins every direct dependency with `==`.
  `requirements.lock.txt` is the fully resolved set including transitive
  dependencies, and installation for reproduction is from the lock file, not the
  loose file. Permitted libraries for Part 3: `pandas`, `numpy`, `scipy`,
  `statsmodels`, `matplotlib`. Adding a dependency requires an `assumptions.md`
  entry.
- **Seed policy:** exactly one seed constant, `RANDOM_SEED = 20260910`, defined in
  one module and imported everywhere it is needed. Every random process constructs
  an explicit generator from it; no global or legacy seeding, and no reliance on
  default RNG state. If independent streams are needed, they are derived from that
  one seed by spawning, never by inventing a second literal. A second seed literal
  anywhere in the repository is a defect. Consequence: same seed, same resample
  count, same `numpy` version ⇒ bit-identical interval endpoints.
- **Clean-checkout rule:** from a fresh clone, this sequence and nothing else
  reproduces every number, table, and figure in `reports/`: create the environment at
  the pinned interpreter version → install from the lock file → fetch the raw CSV and
  verify its SHA-256 → run the entrypoint. No manual step, no notebook, no
  copy-paste.
- **Every number in prose must exist in a generated output file.** A figure typed
  directly into markdown is a defect, because it cannot be checked and it will not
  survive a re-run.
- **Quoted and derived figures, distinguished — added in v1.6.** The rule above
  catches invention. It does not catch **miscounting**, and Part 1 proved it: a
  hand-written "20 ineligible cells" passed a pre-commit audit that verified every
  prose figure appears verbatim in a committed table, because "20" appeared in the
  tables as an unrelated figure. The true count was **5**. The generated tables were
  right; the error was in prose assembled around them, and a wrong number built from
  correct-looking parts satisfies a check designed to catch fabricated ones.
  - A **quoted** figure appears verbatim as a cell of a committed table. The rule
    above covers it.
  - A **derived** figure is computed from committed tables rather than read from one:
    a count of rows meeting a condition, a sum, a difference, a ratio, a percentage
    across tables, an extremum, or any "*N* of *M*" statement.
  - **Every derived figure in prose must be turned into a quoted one.** The renderer
    computes it and emits it as its own cell in a committed table —
    `outputs/tables/<part>_report_figures.csv`, one row per figure carrying an id,
    the value, a one-line definition, and the source tables it was computed from —
    and the prose quotes that cell. After that there is no second class of figure for
    an audit to miss.
  - **The audit checks the declared cell, not the corpus.** Each prose figure is
    tagged with the table and column it comes from, and the audit verifies **that
    cell**. A number that appears somewhere in the tables but not in its declared
    cell **fails**. Verbatim-presence-anywhere is the weak match that let "20"
    through.
  - **Exempt numerals**, enumerated so the rule is checkable: dates and date ranges;
    section numbers; `ARCHITECTURE.md` and `assumptions.md` references; thresholds
    quoted from this document; and counts of this document's own items.
  - Rejected: **a second-pass recount** — that is the check that just failed, run
    again by the party that wrote the prose. Rejected: **forbidding derived figures
    in prose** — "5 of the 48 cells are null" is exactly the sentence a reader needs,
    and banning it pushes the arithmetic onto the reader. Rejected: **asserting
    derived figures in the verify step without emitting them** — the number would
    then exist in code and in prose but in no committed artefact, so traceability
    breaks and a reader cannot check it without running the code.
- **Committed outputs must match a fresh run — with the guarantee stated at two
  different strengths, because one of them cannot honestly be promised.**
  - **Tables and JSON: byte-identical.** Every file under `outputs/tables/` and
    `outputs/run_manifest.json` must reproduce byte-for-byte from a clean checkout
    on any machine, and the self-verification step enforces it. The only exempt
    field is the manifest's run timestamp.
  - **Figures: not byte-identical across machines, and the document will not claim
    otherwise.** Matplotlib does not produce byte-identical PNGs across versions,
    platforms, or font configurations, so a byte-identity rule for figures would be
    a reproducibility claim that the first re-run on another machine falsifies. The
    guarantee instead is: **(i)** every figure is generated from a committed table
    in `outputs/tables/`, so the numbers and labels behind it are byte-identical
    even when the pixels are not, and no figure is ever the sole record of a value;
    and **(ii)** re-running on the **same machine with the same lock file**
    reproduces byte-identical PNGs.
  - To make **(ii)** hold and keep figures diff-friendly, the implementation must:
    set the `Agg` backend explicitly; fix figure size, DPI, and the font family with
    a declared fallback; pass explicit PNG metadata so no creation date is embedded;
    introduce no timestamps, hostnames, run IDs, or random jitter into any figure;
    and derive every plotted value from a table file rather than recomputing it.
  - The manifest records the `matplotlib` version and backend alongside the
    interpreter and package versions, so a pixel difference between machines is
    diagnosable rather than mysterious.
- **Self-verification:** the entrypoint finishes with the checks in §7.7, step 20 —
  re-reading its own outputs, asserting that the §5 data-handling assertions held,
  that reported denominators equal the recorded row counts, and that exactly one
  Stage 2 branch fired in §1.6.

### 7.6 README requirement

The opener is the repository's front door. v1.0 through v1.6 required **exactly three
sentences a product manager could act on** — what was tested and on how many players,
the estimated effect with its interval, the recommendation and the one thing that would
change it. That was right for a repository holding one analysis. It is wrong for one
holding three, and v1.7 replaces it.

**The tension, and how it resolves.** Part 3 produces a recommendation; Parts 1 and 2
produce findings and have no recommendation section at all (§10.5, §10.6). An opener
leading with Part 3's recommendation implies the other two also recommend something,
which is precisely what those sections forbid them from doing — two thirds of the
repository would be misrepresented by its own front page. An opener averaging across
all three says nothing anyone could act on. So **the opener's job changes, and
"actionable" survives the change**: it is no longer *the decision*, it is **the single
most actionable statement each analysis supports, with the population it describes named
in the same sentence**. For Part 3 that statement is a recommendation. For Parts 1 and 2
it is a figure with its population — which is actionable in the way that matters to a
reader deciding what they may quote and of whom.

**Structure: exactly *n* + 2 sentences for *n* analyses** — five, for the three in this
repository — before any heading, badge, or "this repository contains":

1. **The frame.** What the repository holds: the analyses, the datasets, and that these
   are separate analyses rather than one study. No adjectives and no claims.
2. **One sentence per analysis, in repository order** — Part 1, then Part 2, then
   Part 3, not build order. Each states the single most actionable thing that analysis
   supports and **names its population in the same sentence**.
3. **One closing sentence: the limitation that bounds every analysis here**, chosen as
   the one a reader would otherwise wrongly assume away.

Constraints, all checkable:

- **Every sentence names its population.** This is the anti-overclaim device the
  three-sentence version never needed and the longer version cannot do without:
  "classic D7 is 5.67%" and "classic D7 among users with an observed install event is
  5.67%" are different claims, and only the second is true.
- **No sentence for Part 1 or Part 2 may be phrased as a recommendation.** Neither part
  has one, and the opener may not manufacture one by tone.
- **No cross-part inference.** No "and therefore", and no sentence whose subject spans
  two analyses. Synthesis across parts, if it is worth writing at all, is a section
  below the opener and is not the opener.
- **Quoted figures only.** Every figure must appear verbatim as a cell of a committed
  table, and **derived figures are not permitted in the opener at all** (§7.5). If a
  sentence needs one, the relevant part's renderer emits it as a cell first and the
  opener quotes that cell. This constraint is what lets the opener be written without a
  renderer of its own, and it is a real restriction rather than a convenience.
- **At most 45 words per sentence.**
- **No sentence may assert anything that part's own negative-results section forbids.**
- No methodology and no hedging language. Everything else — setup, reproduction steps,
  structure, per-part summaries — goes below the opener.

**Who writes it.** The **architecture session**, which is the only party with all three
analyses in view and the party A-118 reserved the decision for. §8 grants it the opener
region — everything in `README.md` above the first `##` heading — and grants it to no
one else. Each part's own `## Part N` section stays with that part's session, as it
always has.

### 7.7 Execution order

`run_part3.py` runs these steps in this order, and this list is the definition of
that order — §7.5 places order in the entrypoint, and this is what the entrypoint
must contain. Each step is labelled with what a failure does:

- **hard stop** — raise and exit; no outputs are written.
- **downgrade** — set the R0 integrity flag and **continue to completion**, with the
  recommendation withheld per §2.4.
- **finding** — record it in the outputs and continue normally.

1. **Resolve and record the environment** — interpreter version, resolved package
   versions, backend, seed. *Hard stop* if the interpreter minor is not 3.12 or a
   pinned package version does not match the lock file.
2. **Verify the input** — locate the raw CSV and check its SHA-256 against the value
   recorded in `assumptions.md`. *Hard stop* on a missing file or a mismatch.
3. **Load the raw CSV with every column as text**, no dtype inference. *Hard stop*
   on a read failure.
4. **Record the raw row count and column set**, and assert the column set is exactly
   the five expected columns. *Hard stop* on mismatch.
5. **Structural validation of every value against its column's token sets** (§5.4)
   — `version` against the arm set plus the missing set, each retention flag against
   its true, false and missing sets, and `sum_gamerounds` against
   non-negative-integer-or-missing. *Hard stop* on any value outside its sets, with
   the column, the offending value and its row count named in the exception. This is
   the **single structural gate**: past this point no token value can stop the run,
   and every remaining fault is counted rather than fatal.
6. **Coerce `version`, then the retention flags**, to their three-state
   representations (§5.4) — safe because step 5 passed. A recognised missing marker
   becomes the explicit missing state; no row is dropped and no value is guessed.
7. **Partition off unassignable rows** — those whose `version` is missing — and
   record their count and share. *Finding*; *downgrade* if they exceed 0.1% of raw
   rows (§2.2).
8. **SRM test on the assignment population** — exact binomial plus the chi-square
   statistic, on the denominator defined in §2.2, before any other exclusion.
   Reported unconditionally as a *finding*; *downgrade* if p < 0.001.
9. **Post-coercion assertion group** (§5.4) — each retention column's non-missing
   values exactly {`True`, `False`}, the three state counts summing to the raw row
   count, per-state counts matching their pre-coercion source-token counts,
   `version`'s non-missing values exactly the arm set with the unassignable count
   equal to its missing count, and `sum_gamerounds` a non-negative integer among its
   non-missing values. *Hard stop* on any failure.
10. **Uniqueness check on `userid`**, classifying duplicates by case (§5.2). Counts
    recorded as *findings*; *downgrade* if cross-arm duplicates exceed 0.1% of raw
    rows.
11. **Missing-value check on the retention flags** (§5.3) — reachable because step 6
    coerced missing markers to an explicit state instead of raising on them.
    Exclusion counts recorded as *findings*, differential missingness between arms
    recorded regardless; *downgrade* if missing values in the primary metric exceed
    0.5% of either arm.
12. **Build the per-metric analysis populations** and record each denominator, then
    report the post-exclusion arm counts as the descriptive differential-exclusion
    check (§2.2) — no test, no threshold.
13. **Power computation on the primary metric** — the three quantities in §3, using
    the observed control rate and observed arm sizes.
14. **Primary inference** — pooled-variance z-test, unpooled analytic interval, and
    the seeded stratified bootstrap with 10,000 resamples giving the percentile
    interval and its Monte Carlo standard error.
15. **Conflict evaluation** (§4.3) — sets precondition R6 if it fires. *Finding.*
16. **Guardrail inference** on `retention_1` — the same estimators, interval only,
    no test decision (§1.2).
17. **Engagement descriptives on `sum_gamerounds`** — with and without the extreme
    row, plus median and the stated winsorised summary (§5.1).
18. **Evaluate the decision pipeline** — Stage 1 preconditions, then Stage 2 branch,
    then Stage 3 modifiers (§1.6) — recording which rule fired at each stage.
19. **Write outputs** — tables, then figures generated from those tables, then
    `outputs/run_manifest.json`.
20. **Self-verification** — re-read the written outputs and assert that the step 9
    assertions held, that every reported denominator equals the row count recorded
    for it, that exactly one Stage 2 branch fired, and that the recorded
    recommendation equals the one the pipeline in step 18 produces. *Hard stop* on
    any failure.

Three ordering constraints are load-bearing rather than incidental. The **structural
gate at step 5** runs before any statistic exists, so no number is ever computed —
let alone reported — from a file that turns out not to be the file this document
describes. The **SRM test at step 8** runs before the duplicate and missing-value
handling at steps 10 and 11, which is what makes the §2.2 denominator rule true in
the code rather than only in prose. And the **decision pipeline at step 18** runs
after every downgrade flag can have been set, so R0 is known before any branch is
evaluated.

The steps are also arranged so that **every hard stop precedes every reported
number**: steps 1–6 and the assertion group at step 9 are the only places the run
can terminate on the data, and all of them sit ahead of step 19's output write. A
run that reaches step 19 has already established that the file is the file.

### 10.1 The query-budget protocol — recon, and now the build sessions

**Owner: a dedicated recon session**, listed in §8, separate from both the completed
Part 3 session and the future Part 1 and Part 2 build sessions. The reason it is not
simply given to the Part 1 session is structural rather than tidy-minded: a session
that has just seen the shard ranges, null rates and event volumes, and is then asked
to write metric definitions, is a session choosing definitions with the answers in
front of it. Separating the two makes the handover through the architecture session
(§10.4) mandatory instead of a matter of discipline — the same reasoning that keeps
`ARCHITECTURE.md` read-only to implementation sessions (§8).

**Recon SQL is committed**, in the `00`–`09` range reserved for it in §7.2. The
recon's findings become the factual basis for two-thirds of the project's
specification, and a fact whose query nobody can read is an assertion. Filing it
under `90`–`99` was rejected — see the note in §7.2.

**Budget rule.** The BigQuery sandbox allows 1 TiB of query processing per month with
no payment method attached, and the project must stay re-runnable at zero cost
(§7.5). Therefore:

**Ceilings, reset in v1.5 from what the recon measured.** The original 50 GiB and
200 GiB were set against a concern item 12 disproved: that the 10 MiB billing minimum
applied **per shard**, which would have made a 114-shard wildcard cost 1.14 GiB before
reading anything. It does not — the minimum is **per query**, and the 114-shard
wildcard billed **274 MiB** (A-107). The whole recon spent **4.90 GiB of 200 GiB**,
2.45% of a ceiling set for a risk that was not real, and dry-run estimates equalled
bytes processed **exactly** on every protocol query. So the ceilings below are set from
the measured cost of this dataset rather than from the superseded worry:

| Ceiling | Value | Why this number |
|---|---|---|
| Per query | **4 GiB** billed | A full-column scan of the **entire** 114-shard range costs **3.87 GiB** (A-107). 4 GiB is therefore the price of the most expensive query anyone could legitimately write against this table. A query estimating above it is reading more than the whole dataset, which takes a join blow-up or a missing predicate — exactly the mistake class a ceiling should catch, and nothing a correct query can trip. |
| Per build session | **20 GiB** billed | Roughly five full-table scans, against a part that should need well under 2 GiB if it selects columns at all — the recon did thirteen queries, one of them a deliberate full-column scan, for 4.90 GiB. |
| Both build sessions together | **40 GiB** billed | Recon plus both builds plus one full re-run of everything then fits inside about 90 GiB — under 9% of the sandbox's 1 TiB monthly allowance, which is what keeps §7.5's zero-cost re-runnability comfortable rather than marginal. |

- **Billed bytes are predictable, so predict them.** A-107 established the exact rule
  **billed = max(10 MiB, ceil(processed → whole MiB))**, holding without exception on
  all 13 ledger rows, with estimates equal to processed on every protocol query. The
  gate is therefore no longer a tolerance band but a **prediction**: before a query
  runs, predicted billed = `max(10 MiB, ceil(estimate → MiB))`.
- **Anything answerable from table metadata is answered from metadata, at zero cost.**
  A-107 showed `size_bytes` predicts billed bytes to within the MiB rounding, so any
  question about size, row counts or shard inventory is a metadata question, never a
  scan.
- **Every query is dry-run first.** The estimate is recorded before the query is
  executed, and the query runs only if the estimate is within the per-query ceiling
  *and* within the remaining total. The estimate is the only number available before
  execution, so it is what the pre-flight gate uses — and it is used for nothing
  else.
- **Reconciliation: the running total is kept in actual bytes billed, not in
  estimates.** After each query executes, the **actual bytes billed** and bytes
  processed are read from the job statistics and recorded in the recon results file
  alongside the estimate, together with the divergence — billed minus estimated, as
  an absolute figure and as a ratio. The running total accumulates the actuals. The
  reason the two cannot be assumed equal: BigQuery bills a minimum per table
  referenced regardless of the estimate, which for a wildcard query touching many
  shards can dominate a cheap query's cost outright; and for wildcard queries
  filtered on `_TABLE_SUFFIX`, what a dry run predicts and what execution bills can
  diverge depending on how shard pruning actually resolves. A pass that tracked only
  estimates could believe it had spent 180 GiB while the account recorded materially
  more — or materially less.
- **Every divergence is a finding, in both directions, on every query.** Not only
  large ones. The divergence is direct evidence about how the wildcard filter is
  being planned, which is the cost model item 12 has to produce, so under-billing
  matters as much as over-billing: if execution prunes shards better than the dry run
  predicts, a per-day cost model built on estimates overstates the Part 1 and Part 2
  build cost and could force a materialised extract that is not actually needed.
- **Halt rule.** The session **stops** if any of these fires:
  - **Total ceiling breached in actuals** — accumulated billed bytes reach the
    session's total ceiling. This is checked in **both** directions: a query may not
    run if `predicted billed + accumulated actuals` would exceed the ceiling, and the
    session stops if accumulated actuals reach it after a query returns. v1.3 and
    v1.4 lacked this condition entirely, so a query could clear the pre-flight gate,
    bill under the per-query ceiling, sit inside the divergence band, and still push
    the running total past the total ceiling with nothing firing.
  - **The billing rule fails** — billed bytes differ from
    `max(10 MiB, ceil(estimate → MiB))` by more than **1 MiB**. The rule held on
    13 of 13 measured jobs, so a failure means the cost model is wrong, and every
    subsequent gate decision rests on it.
  - a single query's **billed** bytes exceed the **per-query ceiling** of 4 GiB,
    whatever it was estimated at.

  A halt is not a finding to note and move past. The pre-flight gate is the only cost
  control the pass has, and it is estimate-based; once the estimate is demonstrably
  not predictive at that magnitude, every subsequent gate decision is unreliable, and
  continuing means spending an unknown amount on the strength of a number already
  known to be wrong. The cost of halting is small — recon is at most ten queries —
  and the recovery is to record the divergence and what it implies about the planner
  as a finding, then escalate to the architecture session, which either re-scopes the
  remaining items or re-specifies the queries so their cost is predictable (for
  example against an explicit shard list rather than a suffix predicate). Continuing
  and hoping, and raising a ceiling to accommodate the overrun, are both forbidden.
  A divergence in the **under**-billing direction never halts the pass: it is
  recorded, it changes item 12's conclusion, and it costs nothing.
- **Shard inventory comes from table metadata, not from scanning rows** — item 1
  costs no query bytes and must not be answered with a row scan.
- Every query against `events_*` restricts the shard range explicitly and selects
  only the columns it needs. A query that reads a column no item asks about is a
  defect regardless of what it costs.
- **If a planned query exceeds a ceiling it is not run.** It is narrowed — a shorter
  shard range, fewer columns, aggregation pushed earlier, or a single-shard sample —
  and dry-run again. If it still exceeds after narrowing, the item is recorded as
  **unanswered**, with the reason and the estimate, and escalated to the
  architecture session, which either re-scopes the item or accepts the unknown and
  writes it into Parts 1 and 2 as a stated limitation. Raising a ceiling, enabling
  billing, or running a query whose estimate was never taken are all forbidden.
- **If the remaining total — in actuals — falls below the per-query ceiling, the
  session stops** and reports what it has, rather than running a partial query set.
- **Every session keeps the ledger.** `outputs/tables/<part>_budget_ledger.csv`, in
  A-094's columns, is a required output of each build session exactly as it was of the
  recon. A part whose byte spend is not in a committed ledger has not accounted for
  itself.

**Output artifacts — all three, with an explicit precedence:**

| Artifact | Role |
|---|---|
| `outputs/tables/recon_*.csv` | The raw query results. **Source of truth for every number.** |
| `reports/recon_ga4_sample.md` | The checklist rendered for a human: one row per item, with the question, the artefact, the observed value, a verdict of *answered* / *problematic* / *unanswered*, the query file, and both the dry-run estimate and the actual bytes billed. A rendering, not an authority. |
| `assumptions.md` `finding` entries | One per item whose answer a later decision rests on. **Source of truth for the interpretation** — what the number means for the specification — but never for the number itself. |

Where the report or a finding entry disagrees with the CSV, **the CSV wins and the
discrepancy is a defect.** The split exists because `assumptions.md` is append-only
prose that cannot be regenerated, so it must not be the authority for a figure a
re-run could change; and because the interpretation is a judgement that belongs in
the append-only record rather than in a regenerated file.

Each recon result file carries a **provenance line** — shard range covered, query job
date, dry-run estimate, **actual bytes billed**, and row count — because its source
is an external table rather than a checksummed local file. Both byte figures are
carried, not just one: the estimate is what authorised the query and the billed
figure is what it cost, and a reader who can see only one cannot tell whether the
budget was tracked correctly. Whether that replaces or merely supplements §7.5's
byte-identity rule for BigQuery-derived outputs is §9's open question 7.

### 10.3 Part 2's scope is decided by item 9, by rule — and the rule has fired

**Outcome, recorded before the specification that follows it.** Item 9 returned
**27 revenue-positive `in_app_purchase` events from 27 distinct users**, against a
§10.3 denominator of **15,175** users (A-104). Threshold 1 is missed by a factor of
**37** (27 against 1,000); threshold 2 is missed by a factor of **2.8** (27 users =
**0.178%**, against 0.5% = 75.9 users). Both missed, so the rule selects the
**re-scope branch: Part 2 is a progression funnel only** (§10.6). No judgement was
exercised at this step — the rule was written in v1.3, the denominator named in v1.4,
and both predate the query.

**One qualification the re-scope statement must carry.** `event_value_in_usd` is
**absent from 15 of the 114 shards**, and of the 27 purchase events only **24** carry
a strictly positive value in that field while all 27 carry a positive `price`
parameter. So the revenue count is a **lower bound**, not an exact figure, and Part 2
must say so in those terms. It changes nothing about the outcome — 27 could be off by
an order of magnitude and still miss 1,000 by a factor of 3 — and saying "27, and
that is a floor" costs nothing, whereas presenting a floor as a census is the kind of
small overclaim that a reader who knows this dataset would catch first.



Item 9 decides whether Part 2 is *funnel and monetization* or *progression funnel
only*. That decision is made by a rule fixed here, before the query is run, for the
same reason §1.6 fixes the gate decision before the result: otherwise the scope of
Part 2 would be chosen after seeing which version of it looks better.

**"Populated" means both of the following, measured over the full shard range from
item 1, counting only revenue-positive purchase events as defined in item 9:**

- **at least 1,000 revenue-positive purchase events**, and
- **at least 0.5% of the denominator population defined below with at least one such
  event.**

**The denominator, named literally.** The denominator is the count of **distinct
`user_pseudo_id` values with at least one event of any kind in the full shard range
established by item 1**. Users with events but **no** `first_open` are **in** the
denominator. No trailing-window exclusion is applied. The numerator uses the **same
identifier over the same shard range** — distinct `user_pseudo_id` values with at
least one revenue-positive purchase event.

This is fixed here, not chosen later, because item 2 exists precisely to establish
that the distinct user count, the count with a `first_open`, and the gap between them
are three different numbers on this dataset, and item 11 will show whether the
counting unit changes conversion denominators. A rule whose denominator was selected
after those numbers were visible would be the failure this subsection opens by saying
it exists to prevent — the same defect §2.2 had before v1.2 named the SRM
denominator literally.

Justified on measurement grounds, and every choice resolves in the direction that
makes the threshold **harder** to clear:

- **All users, not installers only.** The broadest population gives the largest
  denominator and therefore the hardest bar. Restricting to users with a `first_open`
  would inflate the payer share by exactly the size of the gap item 2 measures, which
  is the one direction a scope gate must never be easy in. It would also be
  inconsistent: a purchase by a user with no `first_open` would count in the
  numerator while that user was excluded from the denominator.
- **Independent of every unsettled definition.** This denominator is computable from
  one unambiguous artefact — distinct identifier values in a shard range — and needs
  nothing from items 2, 3, 4 or 5. Install attribution, day boundaries, timezone
  semantics and cohort grain are all unresolved, and a gate must not rest on
  definitions the recon has not returned yet.
- **`user_pseudo_id` regardless of what item 4 finds.** It is the identifier a GA4
  export always carries, so the denominator exists whatever `user_id`'s null rate
  turns out to be. If `user_id` proves populated and cross-device deduplication
  becomes possible, `user_pseudo_id` over-counts people — one person on two devices
  counts twice — which again makes the bar harder, so the conservative reading holds
  either way.
- **No trailing-window exclusion.** A user installing on the final shard has little
  chance to purchase, but this is a sample-adequacy count over the whole extract
  rather than a cohort metric, and any such exclusion would shrink the denominator
  and make the bar easier.

**The denominator is not re-selected after item 2, item 4 or item 13 reports.** If
one of them shows this denominator is unusable — `user_pseudo_id` obfuscated to a
placeholder, say — that is a `challenge` raised **before** the counts are read as a
verdict, resolved by the architecture session, never a substitution made while
looking at the numbers.

| Outcome | Consequence, fixed in advance |
|---|---|
| **Both thresholds met** | Part 2 is specified as **funnel and monetization**, as originally scoped. |
| **Either threshold missed** | Part 2 is **re-scoped to a progression funnel only**. It may not be titled, introduced, or summarised as monetization anywhere — README included — and it must carry an explicit statement that this sample does not support revenue analysis, with the observed counts given. |

Why these numbers, and why both:

- **1,000 events.** Below that, any monetization metric a case study would report —
  conversion by cohort, ARPPU, revenue per install — has cells small enough that a
  single heavy spender or a single unusual day dominates the answer. Presenting that
  as analysis is presenting noise. A genuinely populated sample clears 1,000 events
  without effort, so the threshold only bites on samples that should not be analysed
  this way.
- **0.5% of users.** Casual mobile titles convert in the low single digits of
  percent. A sample with under one payer in 200 is truncated, obfuscated, or not
  representative, and a funnel whose final step holds fewer than 1 in 200 users
  cannot support a per-step comparison between cohorts at all.
- **Both, not either.** They fail differently and both failures matter: 1,000 events
  concentrated in twenty users is volume without coverage, and 0.5% coverage of a
  tiny base is coverage without volume. Requiring both means neither failure mode
  passes by borrowing the other's strength.

The rule is evaluated **once**, from item 9's committed result file, before any
Part 2 section is written. Re-counting after seeing which version of Part 2 reads
better, adjusting the purchase-event set to clear a threshold, or counting
`spend_virtual_currency` toward it, each invalidate the rule — and whichever side
fires, the observed counts and both thresholds are printed in the report, so a
reader can see the rule was applied rather than chosen.

### 10.5 Part 1 — retention and install cohorts

#### 10.5.1 Population: the `first_open` event, 4,319 users

**Cohorts are built on the `first_open` EVENT, and Part 1's population is the 4,319
users who have one.** Not the `first_open_time` user property.

The property covers all **15,175** users (A-109) and the event covers **4,319**
(A-097), so the property is the larger population by a factor of 3.5. It is rejected
anyway, because its semantics are **unverified**: nothing in the recon establishes
whether `first_open_time` is a true install timestamp or a value GA4 assigned when it
first saw the user, and the two differ exactly where it matters — for the 10,856 users
whose install predates the window. Building the cohort structure of a whole part on an
unverified proxy would put every D1, D7 and D30 figure in Part 1 on ground nobody has
checked. **A smaller defensible claim beats a larger unverifiable one**, and that
principle decides it.

**What the choice costs, stated as a cost.** Part 1 discards **71.54%** of the sample's
users. That number is not a footnote: §10.7.2 requires it as the **first table** in
Part 1's report, before any retention figure appears.

**And the exclusion is not random — and the direction is stated here, not promised.**
A user with events but no `first_open` in the window installed before 20180612, or had
the event dropped by the sampling. Either way the 4,319 are systematically **newer**
than the 10,856, so Part 1's cohorts are a recent-installer slice, not a random sample
of players — and the 10,856 are, to that extent, an **established base**. An
established base retains better than new installs. **Therefore Part 1's retention
figures sit below what a whole-population view would show: they understate it.** By
how much is unknown, and no report in this project estimates it; the §10.7.3 probe is
the only available measurement and stays in the negative-results section.

**A general rule, added in v1.6: a stated bias must name its direction.** v1.5 said
"the direction of the bias is knowable" and stopped there, which is a promise of a
disclosure rather than a disclosure — and Part 1's report completed the inference
inline, ending up stronger than the document it was written against. Wherever any part
of this project states that a figure is biased, it names **which way**, or states
explicitly why the direction cannot be determined. "Knowable" is not an answer.

**What Part 1's D1/D7/D30 figures are and are not about.** They describe **users with
an observed `first_open` event inside the window**. They are not about the sample's
users generally (71.54% are absent), not about the game's player base (§10.7.1), and
not comparable to a published retention figure for this title unless that figure uses
the same population, which cannot be checked. Part 1 states this in those terms.

#### 10.5.2 Day boundary and install day

- **Day key: `event_date`.** Not a date recomputed from `event_timestamp` in UTC, and
  not a device-local date. A-098 established that `event_date` equals its own shard
  suffix on **all 5,700,000 rows**, that it sits one day behind the UTC date on
  **33.96%** of rows, and that **zero** rows are ahead of it. A one-sided, consistent
  offset is a fixed non-UTC zone, not noise — consistent with UTC−07:00, and the build
  session **reports the offset it observes** rather than assuming that figure. No
  metric here depends on the zone's identity; every metric depends on the key being
  consistent, which is established.
- **Rejected: a UTC date recomputed from `event_timestamp`.** It would split one local
  day across two keys for a third of all rows, and it forfeits shard pruning, turning
  every query into a full-range scan.
- **Rejected: a device-local date** from `device.time_zone_offset_seconds` (31 distinct
  values, non-null on every row). Defensible in principle, but it gives each user a
  private day boundary, so "day 7" stops being one quantity, and it also forfeits
  pruning.
- **Cost, stated in the report:** day *N* is a property-local day, not a UTC day and
  not the user's own local day.
- **Install day** = the `event_date` of the user's **earliest** `first_open` event. The
  event is near-unique — 4,322 events across 4,319 users, maximum 2 per user, 0.07%
  repeating (A-106) — and taking the earliest makes the 3 repeat cases deterministic
  rather than arbitrary. Install day is **day 0** and is never counted as a retention
  day.

#### 10.5.3 Retention definitions

- **Primary — classic retention.** A user is retained at day *N* if they have **at
  least one event whose `event_date` equals install day + *N* exactly**. D1, D7 and
  D30 use *N* = 1, 7, 30.
- **Secondary — rolling retention**, reported in its own labelled table: retained at
  day *N* if they have at least one event with `event_date` **≥** install day + *N*.
- Both, because they are different quantities that the literature quotes
  interchangeably, and a reader who knows the space will ask which one this is.
  Classic is primary because D1/D7/D30 are conventionally classic, and because rolling
  retention at day *N* is bounded by the observation window in a way classic is not.
  Neither may be presented without its label.
- **Rolling retention is right-censored by construction, and no eligibility rule
  repairs it — added in v1.6.** This is a property of the metric, not a note about one
  report. Rolling at day *N* counts any event on or after install + *N*, so its value
  depends on **how much window remains after that day**. §10.5.5's eligibility rule
  makes a rolling cell **measurable**; it does not make rolling cells **comparable**.
  On this window the observation remaining to a cohort's last installer falls from
  **101 days** (W01) to **3 days** (W15) at D7, and Part 1's rolling D7 tracked it
  down the series from **30.68%** to **9.40%** (A-150).
  - **A weekly rolling series may not be presented or described as a trend.** The
    decline is what a shrinking window produces mechanically, and no behavioural
    reading of it is available.
  - **Pooled rolling figures are a blend of differently censored cohorts**, not an
    estimate of a single quantity, and must be labelled as such wherever they appear.
  - **Any chart of a rolling series names the censoring in its subtitle.**
  - Classic retention is immune: an event on install day + *N* **exactly** needs one
    observable day rather than all of them, which is why eligibility is sufficient for
    classic and only a floor for rolling.
- **Required: window-remaining columns beside every window-bounded secondary cell.**
  Any table reporting a metric whose value depends on the amount of remaining window
  prints **`window_days_remaining_min`** and **`window_days_remaining_max`** beside
  every cell — the days of observation the window leaves after day *N* for the
  cohort's **last** and **first** installer. This was Part 1's own innovation (A-151)
  and v1.6 makes it a requirement, for two reasons: it shows the censoring **gradient**
  directly, so a reader sees that a declining series is an artefact before anyone tells
  them; and because **a cell is ineligible exactly when the minimum falls below 1**, it
  turns §10.5.5's eligibility rule into arithmetic printed on the same row as the NULL
  it explains. Classic tables carry no such column — classic needs the single day
  install + *N* and nothing after it. The requirement is scoped to metrics whose value
  depends on remaining window, so it does not reach a whole-window presence metric such
  as Part 2's funnel (§10.6.2); it would reach any future step defined as "within *N*
  days".
- **Rates carry a 95% Wilson score interval.** Wilson rather than Wald, because
  cohort denominators run from tens to thousands and Wald misbehaves at small *n* and
  near 0 or 1; and rather than a bootstrap, because a closed-form interval on a single
  proportion needs no seed and no resample count.
- **Suppression rule: any cell whose denominator is below 30 is reported as a count
  only** — no rate, no interval. At *n* = 30 a Wilson half-width around a 20% rate is
  already about 15 pp, and a rate nobody should read is better left unprinted than
  printed with a caveat.
- Rates to **two decimal places** in percentage points, exact counts alongside, and
  the **denominator printed next to every rate** (§10.7.6).

#### 10.5.4 Cohort grain: weekly

**Weekly.** Daily is impossible and a single pooled cohort is not a cohort analysis.

- **Daily rejected on measurement grounds.** `first_open` per shard has median **38**,
  minimum **1**, maximum **71**, and **5 of 114 shards carry none at all** (A-100),
  against item 5's stated threshold of 100. A 38-user cohort gives a D7 rate with a
  Wilson half-width of roughly 13 pp, and §10.5.3's suppression rule would blank a
  large share of daily cells outright.
- **Weekly gives ~270 installs per cohort** (4,319 over 16 weeks), where a D7 rate
  near 20% has a Wilson half-width of about 5 pp — readable, and comparable
  week to week.
- **A single pooled cohort is also reported**, as the headline D1/D7/D30 figures,
  because pooled *n* is the most stable number Part 1 has. The weekly cohorts exist
  for the **trend**; the pooled figures are the **level**. Both appear; neither
  replaces the other.
- **Weeks are fixed 7-day blocks anchored at the first shard, not ISO weeks.**
  20180612 is a Tuesday, so ISO weeks would make W01 a 4-day cohort whose denominator
  is not comparable to any other. Anchoring at the first shard gives exactly 16 whole
  weeks plus a 2-day remainder (A-096: 114 days, 114 mod 7 = 2).
- **The 2-day tail, 20181002–20181003, is excluded from every cohort table**, and its
  install count is reported as an excluded figure. Rejected: folding it into W16,
  which would make W16 a 9-day cohort with a denominator ~29% larger than the others
  and silently distort the trend it is there to show.

#### 10.5.5 Observation-window eligibility

A cohort can be measured at day *N* only if its **last** install day plus *N* falls
inside the window, which ends 20181003. The cutoffs are therefore install day ≤
**20181002** for D1, ≤ **20180926** for D7, and ≤ **20180903** for D30.

| Cohort | Install days | D1 | D7 | D30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| W01 | 20180612–20180618 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W02 | 20180619–20180625 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W03 | 20180626–20180702 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W04 | 20180703–20180709 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W05 | 20180710–20180716 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W06 | 20180717–20180723 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W07 | 20180724–20180730 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W08 | 20180731–20180806 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W09 | 20180807–20180813 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W10 | 20180814–20180820 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W11 | 20180821–20180827 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W12 | 20180828–20180903 | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| W13 | 20180904–20180910 | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| W14 | 20180911–20180917 | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| W15 | 20180918–20180924 | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| W16 | 20180925–20181001 | ✓ | — | — |
| tail | 20181002–20181003 | excluded from all cohort tables |

So D1 runs on 16 cohorts, D7 on 15, and D30 on 12.

- **An ineligible cell is printed as explicitly null — not zero, not blank, not
  omitted.** A cohort that cannot be observed for 30 days still appears in the D30
  table, with its install count shown and its rate null. Omitting the row would let a
  reader infer that only 12 cohorts exist; printing zero would be a false retention
  figure of the worst kind.
- **A cohort excluded at one horizon still appears at the horizons it supports.** W16
  is in the D1 table and null in D7 and D30.
- **The pooled figures use a different denominator per horizon**, and each denominator
  is printed beside its rate. Pooling D30 over installs that could not be observed for
  30 days would be the same error as printing zero.
- **"Pooled at horizon *N*" means the union of the cohorts eligible at *N* — per
  cohort, not per install.** Settled in v1.6; A-134 raised that v1.5's "all installs
  eligible at that horizon" admitted both readings and adjudicated neither. So pooled
  D1 = W01–W16, pooled **D7 = W01–W15 exactly**, pooled D30 = W01–W12, and the W16
  installs falling on 20180925–20180926 — **85** of them in Part 1 — are **excluded
  from pooled D7** and reported as an explicit count with their date range in the
  cohort inventory.
  - **Why per cohort.** Under the per-install reading, pooled D7 would contain installs
    from a cohort the weekly table marks `—` at D7: the pooled figure would rest on
    data the weekly table declares unmeasurable, and the two tables would disagree by
    construction. Per cohort keeps pooled and weekly over the **same population**, so
    any difference a reader sees between them is real rather than definitional. It also
    avoids admitting a fractional cohort — two days of W16 — into a figure labelled as
    covering whole cohorts, with no row of its own anywhere.
  - **Only D7 moves.** D1 and D30 are **identical under both readings**, because W12
    ends exactly on the D30 cutoff of 20180903 and W16 ends before the D1 cutoff of
    20181002. The choice is narrow in effect and still worth fixing, because a rule
    that admits two readings will be read both ways across three parts.
  - The word **pooled**, anywhere in §10.5–§10.7, carries this meaning: the union of
    eligible cohorts, never a per-install re-selection.
- **Eligibility is a property of the observation window, not of a metric's primary or
  secondary status — settled in v1.6.** A-135 raised that the 16 / 15 / 12 pattern sits
  in a subsection whose surrounding text is about the primary metric, leaving it
  unstated whether it governs the rolling table. It does. **Observation-window
  eligibility binds every window-bounded metric in this project, present and future** —
  classic retention, rolling retention, and any later metric that requires observing a
  day which may fall outside the window — with ineligible cells printed null through
  the same mechanism in every case. §10.5.7 item 5's "the same two shapes" therefore
  means the same eligibility pattern too. A metric that is not window-bounded, such as
  Part 2's whole-window funnel, is unaffected. And for rolling specifically, eligibility
  is a **floor, not a fix**: it removes the cells with no observable window rather than
  making the remainder equivalent — see §10.5.3.

#### 10.5.6 Session-level analysis is dropped

**Part 1 reports no session-level metric.** No sessions per user, no session length,
no session-gap analysis; the originally sketched `LAG`-over-session-gaps analysis is
dropped. The reason is not difficulty:

- **No session identifier exists anywhere in this export** — not among the 52
  `event_params` keys, not among the 25 `user_properties` keys (A-101, A-109). The
  brief's session-gap analysis assumed a session concept the data does not carry.
- **A `session_start` event exists but cannot stand in for one.** It marks a beginning
  and carries no id, so attributing subsequent events to a session still requires an
  inactivity rule — an invented definition, not a measurement. And its own volume
  argues against trusting it: **74,353 events across 12,261 users, median 2 per user**
  (A-106), against 5,700,000 total events. Two sessions per user cannot describe the
  session structure of a user with hundreds of events, so even the export's own marker
  is unreliable as a boundary.
- **An inactivity threshold was considered and rejected.** Fixing 30 minutes — the GA4
  default — would have been legitimate as a stated convention, but it would produce a
  session count that is an artefact of the threshold, in a part whose other numbers
  are measurements. Publishing both kinds side by side without distinguishing them is
  how a case study loses a reader who knows the difference.
- **Item 11's per-session half stays permanently unanswered** for this dataset, and
  Part 1 says so rather than leaving the absence to be noticed.
- Everything in Parts 1 and 2 is therefore counted **per user** or **per user-shard**.

#### 10.6.2 Population and counting unit

- **Population: all 15,175 distinct `user_pseudo_id` values** with at least one event in
  the shard range. Not Part 1's 4,319.
- **Why not the install cohort.** A funnel step must be reachable by everyone in the
  denominator. Starting at `first_open` would either restrict Part 2 to 28.46% of the
  sample — duplicating Part 1's limitation for no new insight — or produce a
  non-monotone funnel, since **10,166** users started a level while only **4,319** have
  a `first_open`. Part 2's question is progression through content, not acquisition.
- **`first_open` is therefore not a funnel step.** The funnel begins at "present in the
  window".
- **Counting unit: per user.** Per-event is rejected outright:
  `level_start_quickplay` has median 5 events per user, 90th percentile 54, maximum
  24,641, with **81.9%** of its users recording more than one (A-106). A per-event
  funnel would count one heavy player hundreds of times and call it conversion.
- **Per-session is not available** — no session identifier exists (§10.5.6), so item
  11's per-session half is unanswered and Part 2 says so.

#### 10.6.4 Strict counts, raw counts, and out-of-order users

- **The funnel is strict.** Step *k*'s population is users who have **all** of S0…S*k*,
  not users who have S*k*. Monotonicity then holds by construction rather than by luck.
- **Both are reported**: the strict cumulative count and the raw per-step count from
  §10.6.3, side by side, in the same table.
- **Out-of-order users are counted and reported** — users with S2 but not S1, or S3 but
  not S2. A share **above 1.0%** of the step's raw population is a reported finding with
  a stated interpretation, not a number to reconcile away: in an event log it usually
  means an event was dropped by sampling or emitted without its predecessor, and either
  is worth a reader's attention.
- **Step-to-step conversion is reported on the strict population**, with the denominator
  printed beside every rate, and a **95% Wilson interval** on each rate (§10.5.3's
  reasoning applies unchanged).

`level_complete_quickplay` plus `level_fail_quickplay` sum to 328,123 — a shortfall of
21,606 events, **6.18%** of `level_end_quickplay`. Part 2 reports this reconciliation at
both event and user level, and it is **not** silently reconciled and **not** used to
redefine S2. (Those three figures are pre-de-duplication, as v1.5 quoted them from the
recon; a report de-duplicates first (§10.7.4), so its event counts sit marginally below
them by exactly the duplicate rows removed, and both bases are carried in the
reconciliation table — A-166.)

**The user-level comparison, settled in v1.7.** A-164 raised that this section required
the reconciliation "at both event and user level" while fixing only the event-level
arithmetic, and that the event formula does not translate: a user holding both a
complete and a fail appears in both outcome counts, and **4,052** users on this export
do. Three readings were available, and **reading 1 governs**:

| Reading | Definition | On this export |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — governs** | Users with `level_end_quickplay` and **neither** outcome, over the users with S2 | **202 of 8,168 = 2.47%** |
| 2 — rejected | `(users(complete) + users(fail) − users(end)) / users(end)`, the literal translation | +47.15%, an **excess** |
| 3 — rejected | Reading 1's numerator over the users with **either** outcome | 202 of 7,967 = 2.54% |

- **Why reading 1.** The event-level figure measures **ends that carry no outcome**.
  A user-level figure is a reconciliation *of it* only if it measures the same thing at
  user grain, and reading 1 is exactly that — the same population, counted in users
  instead of events. The two shares, 6.18% and 2.47%, are **two views of one population,
  not two findings**, and a report must say so.
- **Why not reading 2.** It measures how many users hold **both** outcomes, which is a
  different quantity that happens to be computable from the same three counts. Its sign
  is governed by the overlap rather than by the gap, so as soon as any user holds both
  outcomes the statistic turns positive — **a shortfall trigger tested against it can
  never fire for the reason it was written**. That is a structural defect, not an
  artefact of this export.
- **Why not reading 3.** Its denominator is the users with either outcome, and its
  numerator is the users with **neither** — so numerator and denominator are
  **disjoint**. It is a ratio between two non-overlapping sets, not a share of anything.
  The arithmetic is worth seeing: 7,967 users have an outcome, of whom exactly **1** has
  no end, so 7,966 + 202 = 8,168, which closes against reading 1's denominator and not
  against reading 3's.
- **The 1.0% trigger applies to reading 1 and to nothing else.**

**What a firing reconciliation trigger obliges — specified in v1.7.** v1.5 said only
"a finding with a stated interpretation", which left the content of that interpretation
to the build session — the gap §10.7.7 exists to prevent elsewhere. When the trigger
fires, the report must state all of:

1. **The asymmetry and its direction, in counts, both ways** — how many ends carry no
   outcome, and how many outcomes carry no end. **The asymmetry is the finding, not the
   percentage**: on this export essentially every user with an outcome records the
   preceding end (1 exception in 7,967) while 202 ends carry none, so whatever is
   happening affects ends and not outcomes. The share is the magnitude; the direction is
   the result.
2. **The components of all three readings**, so a reader can rebuild any of them — users
   per outcome, users holding both, the union, and the outcome-without-end count.
3. **The candidate explanations the export cannot distinguish between** — at minimum
   abandonment mid-attempt, an outcome type the event vocabulary does not name, and loss
   of the outcome event in the sample — together with an explicit statement that the
   report does not choose between them.
4. **That the step is not redefined**, and that no funnel figure moves as a result.
5. **That the event-level and user-level shares are two views of one population.**

And may **not** conclude: a cause; a data-quality verdict on the export as a whole; an
estimate of how many users "really" completed or failed; or any correction applied to
any count. **A firing trigger is a licence to say more, never to change anything.**

**Below the trigger**, the reconciliation is still printed at both levels with all
components, and a single sentence stating the figures discharges the requirement — the
five obligations above do not apply.

### 10.7 What both parts owe the reader

#### 10.7.1 The downsampling disclosure

**Every shard holds exactly 50,000 rows** (A-096). That uniformity is not traffic; it is
a sampling cap. Both reports carry this as a top-level section, and the README carries
one sentence of it:

- Parts 1 and 2 describe a **50,000-events-per-day sample** of this property, not the
  game's player base.
- **No absolute count may be presented as a measure of real traffic** — not installs per
  week, not users, not events per day.
- **No growth or trend claim may be made from volume.** A rise in installs per week
  across the 16 cohorts is a change in what the sample captured, not a change in
  acquisition. Rates within a cohort are the only quantities the sample supports.
- **The sampling fraction per day is unknown and may not be uniform**, so even
  comparisons of *rates* across weeks could be affected if the sample was drawn
  differently on different days. This is unresolvable from the data and belongs in each
  report's negative-results section, not in a hedge attached to one chart.

#### 10.7.2 Required report structure

Each report opens with, in this order: the **population reconciliation** table, the
**downsampling disclosure**, then its results. Part 2 adds the §10.6.1 re-scope
statement before its results.

Each report ends with a **"What this does not support"** section, in the shape §6
requires of Part 3 — placed after the results and before any recommendation, not a
caveats footnote — covering at minimum: what the 50,000-row cap forbids (§10.7.1); for
Part 1, the 71.54% exclusion, its non-random direction, and what the figures are not
about (§10.5.1); for Part 2, that no revenue question is answerable and no session
question is answerable; the absence of any cross-device identity (§10.7.3); and for
each, what measurement would be required to answer the question properly — naming the
measurement, never "further research is needed".

#### 10.7.3 Identity, and the `first_open_time` sensitivity check

- **`user_id` is NULL on all 5,700,000 rows** (A-099). `user_pseudo_id` is the only
  identifier, so **"a user" means a device-install throughout both parts**. One person
  on two devices is two users; a reinstall may be a new user. **No cross-device claim,
  no unique-people claim, and no deduplication of people is possible** — and both
  reports say so rather than using the word "users" as if it meant people.
- **Part 1 must attempt one sensitivity check and report the outcome either way.**
  Derive install day from the `first_open_time` user property for all 15,175 users and
  recompute pooled D7. **Run the check only if** `first_open_time`'s derived date equals
  the `first_open` event's `event_date` for **≥ 99.0%** of the 4,319 users who have
  both — otherwise the property's semantics are not established even for the
  overlapping population, the check is **omitted**, and the observed agreement share is
  reported instead.
- **The zone the date is derived in — specified in v1.6, with defined behaviour when
  nothing fits.** Select the whole-hour UTC offset with the **highest row-level
  agreement with `event_date`** across candidate offsets, report that agreement share
  and the **margin over the runner-up**, and on an exact tie take the **lower** of the
  tied offsets' agreement shares against the 99.0% gate. **This rule cannot select
  nothing:** a highest-agreement criterion always selects, and the selection carries
  its own quality measure. If the best available agreement is itself below 99.0%, the
  selection still stands and **the check is omitted by its own gate above**, with the
  agreement share reported as the reason — the gate does that work, never the zone
  rule.
  - **Why the obvious stricter criterion is wrong.** A rule requiring an offset
    consistent with `event_date` on **all 5,700,000 rows** is unsatisfiable by correct
    data: `event_date` is the export's own day stamp, not a field derivable from
    `event_timestamp`, and A-143 found the feasible interval for a single constant
    offset **empty by 93 seconds**, with 1,223 rows — **0.0215%** — sitting across a
    local midnight. §10.5.2's argument for the day key never claimed derivability, only
    **consistency**, which is established. A criterion stricter than this document's own
    claim is not conservatism; it is a rule correct data cannot satisfy, and it selects
    nothing — which is how Part 1's first pass omitted a required check for a reason
    having nothing to do with the field under test.
  - **The selection must be independent of the figure the check produces.** Row-level
    agreement with `event_date` is a property of the export, not an output of the
    sensitivity check, which is what makes it a legitimate criterion. A criterion whose
    value could be influenced by the number the step will report is never permitted
    (§10.7.7). When it runs, the figure goes in the negative-results section,
  labelled as a robustness check on an unverified field, with the agreement share beside
  it. It never appears in a results table and never becomes a headline. It exists to
  answer one question a reader will ask — what does discarding 71.54% cost — and it
  cannot answer it authoritatively, which is why its placement is fixed.

#### 10.7.4 Duplicate rows

**De-duplicate on (`user_pseudo_id`, `event_name`, `event_timestamp`) before any count,
in both parts, and report the 207 rows removed** (A-103, 36 ppm).

This is the opposite of Part 3's rule, and deliberately so. §5.1 keeps a suspicious row
because removing it after randomisation breaks intention-to-treat. There is no
randomisation and no ITT here: a duplicated log row is not an observation of anything,
and while 36 ppm cannot move a rate, distinct-user counts and per-user event counts are
both sensitive to exact duplication. The contrast is stated in each report so it does
not read as an inconsistency between parts.

#### 10.7.5 Segmentation

**Permitted dimensions** (A-108): `geo.country` (155 values, 0% null), `platform`
(2 values, 0% null), `device.category` (2 values, 0% null), `device.language`
(229 values, 0% null), `app_info.version` (34 values, 0% null).

**Dropped: `geo.region`** — 88.57% null, failing item 13's 50% test.

**Dropped: `traffic_source.name`, `.medium`, `.source`** — they pass the null and
distinct-count tests but are **99.91%, 99.79% and 99.39%** concentrated in two buckets,
one of which is a placeholder (`(direct)`, `(none)`) in each case. A dimension with
essentially one real bucket cannot distinguish cohorts: any segment difference would
compare under 1% of events against the rest and read as acquisition analysis while
being noise. **Traffic source is reported once, as a single descriptive line stating the
concentration, to document why it is not segmented** — and no metric is segmented by it.

Rules, all numeric:

- **Attribution is by the user's earliest event row**, ties broken by lowest
  `event_timestamp` then by alphabetically lowest `event_name`. One deterministic rule
  for both parts.
- **Report the non-constant share for every permitted dimension, and apply the triggers
  to each — generalised in v1.6.** For each of the five permitted dimensions, report the
  share of users whose value is not constant across their events. Above **5.0%** for a
  dimension, that dimension's segmentation is reported with a caveat naming the share;
  above **25.0%**, that dimension is **dropped**. The triggers apply **per dimension,
  independently**, not to `geo.country` alone as v1.5 had it.
  - **Why.** A-136 raised that v1.5 attached the constancy test to the dimension where
    instability is **least** likely and omitted it from the one where it is **most**
    likely: app versions change over a 114-day window by nature — that is what a
    release is. Part 1 measured both rather than inventing the missing gate, and the
    worry was real in direction and small in size: **1.97%** non-constant for
    `app_info.version` against **1.62%** for `geo.country` (A-148). The dimension
    without the test did vary more, and at that magnitude it cost this dataset nothing.
    The rule is generalised because being right by luck on one export is not a reason to
    keep a rule that was attached to the wrong end.
  - **Name what earliest-event attribution actually measures.** Under §10.7.5's
    attribution rule, "segment by `app_info.version`" means **version at install**, and
    "segment by `device.language`" means **language at first observed event** — both
    defensible quantities, neither the one the phrase suggests. Any report segmenting by
    a dimension whose earliest-event value differs in meaning from "the user's value"
    says so in those words, in the table's own caption.
- **Segment floors.** Part 1: a segment is reported individually only with **≥ 100
  users** in the eligible install population (of 4,319). Part 2: **≥ 200 users** at S0
  (of 15,175) — S3 retains 37.40%, so 200 at entry leaves about 75 at the last step,
  which is the smallest final cell worth printing.
- **Below the floor, segments are pooled into one "Other (n segments)" row** with its
  own count. Never dropped silently, never reported individually.
- **Segmentation applies to pooled cohorts only, never crossed with the weekly
  cohorts.** 4,319 installs over 16 weeks and 5 countries is about 54 users per cell,
  below §10.5.3's suppression floor for most cells.

#### 10.7.6 Numbers and reproducibility

§7.5's reproducibility rules and A-080's provenance rule apply unchanged, with three
part-specific points:

- **Every rate carries its denominator**, printed adjacent, and every rate below a
  denominator of 30 is suppressed to a count (§10.5.3).
- **Rates to two decimal places** in percentage points; exact counts as integers; no
  significance stars anywhere (A-031).
- **Derived figures follow §7.5's rule:** a count, sum, difference, ratio, percentage
  across tables, extremum, or "*N* of *M*" statement in prose is emitted as its own
  cell in `outputs/tables/<part>_report_figures.csv` and quoted from there, and the
  audit checks the **declared cell** rather than the corpus.
- **Every number in prose traces to a committed table.** BigQuery-derived outputs are
  exempt from cross-machine byte-identity and carry a provenance sidecar instead
  (A-080, A-093); §9's open question 7 stays open, and item 12's measured figures —
  metadata predicting billed bytes to MiB rounding, 3.87 GiB for the whole table — now
  make a checksummed materialised extract affordable enough to be worth revisiting when
  both parts are done.

#### 10.7.7 When a rule in this document cannot be satisfied

Part 1 hit a rule that **correct data could not satisfy** — A-141's requirement of a
UTC offset consistent with `event_date` on all 5,700,000 rows — and replaced the
criterion itself, substituting **highest** agreement for **consistent on every row**.
The replacement inverts the principle it supersedes, minimum becoming maximum, and it
was written with the results visible. Part 1's report discloses that in its own
section 8.2 and does
not explain it away, and that disclosure is the only reason the result stands. v1.6
turns the episode into a rule.

**A build session may not replace a selection criterion on its own authority.** When a
session finds a rule in this document unsatisfiable by correct data, the response is
fixed:

1. **Stop at that point.** Do not proceed on a substituted criterion.
2. **Append a `challenge` entry** stating the criterion, the evidence that it cannot be
   satisfied, and the margin by which it fails — 93 seconds and an empty feasible
   interval, in A-143's case.
3. **Proceed on the document's own default for a failed gate.** For a gated optional
   step that means **omit the step and report the omission with its reason**. A session
   that omits a step because a rule was unsatisfiable has followed this document, not
   failed it.
4. The **architecture session** then replaces the criterion, and the step runs on a
   later pass.

**Where a replacement is nonetheless made under explicit direction from the project
author**, as A-142's was, three disclosures are mandatory and their absence voids the
result:

- that the replacement **inverts or weakens** the criterion it supersedes, named as
  such;
- that it was **written with the dependent results visible**, stated plainly rather
  than left for a reader to infer from the commit order;
- the **margin** by which the new criterion selects, measured **before** the dependent
  figures exist — A-142's 149,335 rows, computed before the agreement figures the check
  would produce.

**The hard line, in every case.** A criterion may only be replaced by one whose value
**cannot be influenced by the figure the step will produce.** Row-level agreement with
`event_date` is a property of the export and not an output of the sensitivity check,
which is what makes A-142 tolerable rather than fatal — the inversion changed which
zone was chosen, not whether the answer looked good. A criterion that reaches, however
indirectly, for the number the step is about is p-hacking whatever its disclosure, and
no direction and no disclosure makes it admissible.
